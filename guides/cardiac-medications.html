The Nursing Collective

By the end, without notes

  • State the hold parameter for every cardiac class before you state the mechanism
  • Sort any cardiac drug into raises potassium, lowers potassium, or neutral
  • Separate an ACE inhibitor from an ARB using one finding
  • Say which calcium channel blockers work on the heart and which work on vessels

01

What actually gets you

Cardiac pharmacology is not a memory problem. It is a sorting problem. There are about eight classes, and each one has a single number or finding that decides whether the dose goes in.

Points get lost here in three predictable ways. Read these before you read anything else.

You learned the mechanism and skipped the hold parameter

You can say that metoprolol blocks beta-1 receptors. Then a scenario gives you a heart rate of 52 and a due dose, and you freeze.

The question is almost never what does it do. The question is what do you check before you give it. Every class has one gate. Learn the gate first and the mechanism second.

You learned the drugs one at a time

So you missed that they fight over potassium. Loops push it down. ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs and aldosterone antagonists push it up.

Most cardiac patients take drugs from both groups at once. And a low potassium turns a therapeutic digoxin level into a toxic one. That link is the single most tested idea in this topic.

You learned the drug and skipped the teaching

Half of the questions on this topic are teaching questions. They do not test the drug. They test the sentence you say to the patient.

Three carry most of the weight. How nitroglycerin is stored and when to call 911. Why a beta-blocker is never stopped abruptly. Why the ACE cough is expected and what gets swapped in for it.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
One Gate Per Class
Before you memorise a mechanism, write the gate. Beta-blockers and digoxin have a pulse. ACE inhibitors and aldosterone antagonists have a potassium. Nitroglycerin has a systolic pressure. Warfarin has an INR. Learn the gates and most of this topic answers itself.
Bottom Line: Stop studying cardiac drugs as a drug list. Study them as three questions: what do I check first, which way does this move potassium, and what do I tell the patient.
02

The four things

For every class below, you should be able to say four things with the book closed. What it does in one sentence. The clue or the indication that puts it in the scenario. What you check before you give it. The teaching point.

If you cannot say all four out loud, you do not know it yet. Familiarity is not recall.

03

The pressure drugs

Blood pressure is flow times resistance. Every drug here pulls one of those two levers. Beta-blockers cut the flow. The rest relax the vessels.

ACE inhibitors or ARBs

Discrimination axis: the cough.

ACE inhibitors: the prils

Lisinopril, enalapril, ramipril, captopril.

  • Blocks the enzyme that makes angiotensin II
  • Dry hacking cough in a real share of patients
  • Angioedema is rare and is an emergency
  • Potassium goes up
  • First dose can drop the pressure hard

Hold for: potassium above 5.5, symptomatic hypotension, rising creatinine.

ARBs: the sartans

Losartan, valsartan, olmesartan.

  • Blocks the receptor instead of the enzyme
  • No cough, which is the whole reason they get used
  • Angioedema risk is much lower but not zero
  • Potassium goes up the same way
  • Same benefit in heart failure and kidney disease

Hold for: the same three things.

The axis that separates them

The cough. ACE inhibitors let bradykinin build up in the lungs and bradykinin makes you cough. ARBs work further downstream, so nothing builds up.

When a scenario says a patient on lisinopril has a nagging dry cough, the answer is not stop the drug and treat a chest infection. The answer is expected effect, tell the provider, expect a switch to an ARB.

Neither class is given in pregnancy. Both raise potassium. Both are held above 5.5.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Cough Means Swap, Not Stop
A dry cough on an ACE inhibitor is an expected effect and the fix is an ARB. Facial, lip or tongue swelling is a different animal. That is angioedema, the drug stops immediately, and the airway is the priority.

Beta-blockers

Slower rate, softer squeeze, lower demand. That is why they help angina, heart failure and A-fib all at once.

Cardioselective ones sit on beta-1 in the heart. Metoprolol, atenolol, bisoprolol. Nonselective ones also hit beta-2 in the lungs. Propranolol, carvedilol, labetalol, sotalol.

What to knowDetail
Before the doseApical pulse for a full minute and a blood pressure. Hold if the rate is under 60.
Never stop abruptlyReceptors are upregulated. Sudden withdrawal brings rebound tachycardia, hypertension, angina and infarction.
Reactive airwaysNonselective agents can trigger bronchospasm. Cardioselective ones are preferred in asthma and COPD.
DiabetesThey mask the racing heart and shakiness of a low sugar. Sweating still comes through. Teach glucose checks, not symptom watching.
Heart failure dosingStart low, go slow. Carvedilol, metoprolol succinate and bisoprolol are the survival agents.
Coronary spasmAvoided in vasospastic angina. Blocking beta leaves alpha vasoconstriction unopposed.

Calcium channel blockers

Discrimination axis: does the name end in dipine.

Dihydropyridines: vessels

Amlodipine, nifedipine, nicardipine.

  • Act on arterial smooth muscle, not the conduction system
  • Strong blood pressure drop
  • Ankle and foot edema is the classic complaint
  • Headache and flushing
  • Reflex tachycardia, so the pulse can rise

Check: blood pressure and ankles.

Non-dihydropyridines: heart

Diltiazem and verapamil. Only two names to hold.

  • Act on the vessels and on the AV node
  • Used for rate control in A-fib and for SVT
  • Slow the heart, so bradycardia and heart block are real risks
  • Verapamil is famous for constipation
  • Avoided in heart failure with a reduced ejection fraction

Check: blood pressure and pulse, every time.

The axis that separates them

If the name ends in dipine, it works on vessels and may speed the pulse up. If it is diltiazem or verapamil, it works on the heart and slows the pulse down.

That is why diltiazem and verapamil are not routinely paired with a beta-blocker. Two drugs slowing the same node gives you profound bradycardia and heart block.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Dipine Means Vessels
Dipine equals vessels. Diltiazem and verapamil equal the heart. Swollen ankles in a hypertensive patient points at amlodipine. A slow pulse and constipation points at verapamil. Two names carry the whole distinction.

When the pressure is an emergency

These are IV drugs, titrated to effect, on a monitor. The rule that gets tested is the speed of the drop.

DrugWhat it isWhat you watch
NicardipineIV calcium channel blockerPressure and heart rate. Easy to titrate.
LabetalolIV alpha and beta blockerBradycardia. Avoid in decompensated heart failure and severe asthma.
NitroprussideArterial and venous dilator, works in secondsProtect the bag from light. Cyanide toxicity with prolonged or high-dose use.
Do not drop it too fast

Lower the mean arterial pressure by no more than 25 percent in the first hour. Then come down gradually over 24 to 48 hours.

A brain used to a high pressure loses perfusion when you crash it. Dropping the number quickly is how you cause the stroke you were trying to prevent.

Bottom Line: Prils cough, sartans do not, and both raise potassium. Beta-blockers need a pulse before the dose and are never stopped abruptly. Dipine means vessels; diltiazem and verapamil mean the heart.

Diuretics, digoxin and potassium

This is the section that decides your grade. Not because it is hard, but because it is the one place the drugs interact with each other.

Three diuretics, one axis

Discrimination axis: what it does to potassium.

TypeExamplesEffect on K+What you check or teach
LoopFurosemide, bumetanide, torsemideLowersPotassium, pressure, daily weight, intake and output. Push IV doses slowly; a fast push damages hearing.
ThiazideHydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidoneLowersPotassium, sodium, glucose and uric acid. Ask about a sulfa allergy. They lose potency as kidney function falls.
Potassium-sparingSpironolactone, eplerenone, triamtereneRaisesPotassium. Hold above 5.5. Spironolactone causes gynecomastia in men.

Loops and thiazides both waste potassium, so both come with a diet talk. Bananas, oranges, potatoes, spinach, tomatoes.

Potassium-sparing agents go the other way. No salt substitutes, because most of them are potassium chloride. No potassium supplements unless the provider says so.

The axis that separates them

Two groups pull in opposite directions. Loops and thiazides pull potassium down. ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs and aldosterone antagonists push it up.

Cardiac patients take drugs from both groups at once. That is by design; the two effects cancel out. It is also why the potassium sits in the middle of so many questions.

One more difference worth carrying. Loops waste calcium. Thiazides hold onto it.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Two Directions, One Lab
Loops and thiazides drop potassium. ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs and aldosterone antagonists raise it. Hold the ones that raise it above 5.5. And a low potassium turns a therapeutic digoxin level into a toxic one.

ARNI, the one with a washout

Sacubitril with valsartan. It is used instead of an ACE inhibitor in heart failure with a reduced ejection fraction, not alongside one.

Never give it with an ACE inhibitor. The angioedema risk stacks. The ACE inhibitor stops 36 hours before the first ARNI dose, and that washout is the number the exam wants. It raises potassium like the rest of the group.

Digoxin

Digoxin does two things. It makes the squeeze stronger and the rate slower. That combination is useful in heart failure with a fast ventricular rate.

It also has a narrow therapeutic range, which is why it shows up so often.

What to knowDetail
Therapeutic range0.5 to 2.0 ng/mL. Narrow, so a small change matters. In heart failure specifically, current guidelines aim lower, about 0.5 to 0.9.
Before the doseApical pulse for one full minute. Hold if it is under 60 and notify the provider.
The potassium linkLow potassium causes toxicity even when the level reads normal. Check the potassium alongside the level.
Early toxicityAppetite loss, nausea, vomiting, fatigue. These come before anything dramatic.
Classic toxicityYellow or green halos, blurred vision, confusion, bradycardia and new arrhythmias.
AntidoteDigoxin immune Fab.
Normal level, toxic patient

A digoxin level of 1.6 is therapeutic. In a patient whose potassium is 2.9, that same level behaves like poison.

Potassium and digoxin compete for the same site on the pump. Drop the potassium and more digoxin binds. So when a scenario pairs digoxin with a loop diuretic, the potassium is the answer.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Halos and a Slow Pulse
Both digoxin toxicity and worsening heart failure cause fatigue and nausea. Two findings split them. Yellow or green halos with a slow pulse mean toxicity. Rising weight with a fast pulse means the failure is progressing.
Bottom Line: Sort every drug in this section into raises potassium or lowers it. Then remember the one rule that connects them: a low potassium makes a therapeutic digoxin level toxic.
05

Nitrates and the chest pain drugs

Chest pain is a supply and demand problem. Nitrates raise supply and cut demand at the same time. They dilate veins, so less blood comes back to the heart, and they dilate coronary arteries.

The drug is simple. The teaching is what gets tested.

The sublingual nitroglycerin teaching, word for word

  1. 1
    Stop what you are doing and sit or lie down. Nitroglycerin drops blood pressure. People faint standing up.
  2. 2
    Put one tablet under your tongue. Let it dissolve. Do not swallow it and do not chew it.
  3. 3
    Wait 5 minutes. If the pain is not gone, call 911.
  4. 4
    Keep taking tablets while help comes. Up to three tablets in total, five minutes apart, while you wait for EMS.
  5. 5
    Expect a headache. It means the drug is working. Acetaminophen helps and the headache eases over time.
Call at five minutes, not after three tablets

The old teaching was three tablets first, then call. That is not the teaching now.

Call 911 if the first dose has not fixed it after 5 minutes. The other two tablets are taken while EMS is on the way, not instead of calling them.

Storage, and when you withhold it

PointWhy it matters
Original amber glass bottleLight, heat and moisture break the drug down. It stays in its own bottle.
Not in a weekly pill organiserSame reason. Loose in a pocket or a plastic box, it loses potency.
Replace by the date on the bottleAn expired tablet in an emergency is the same as no tablet.
Hold if systolic is under 90The drug lowers pressure further. Check a pressure before every dose.
Hold within 24 to 48 hours of a PDE5 inhibitorSildenafil and tadalafil plus a nitrate cause profound, sometimes fatal hypotension.
Hold in right ventricular infarctThose patients live on preload and the nitrate takes it away.
FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Ask the Erectile Dysfunction Question
Before any nitrate, ask about sildenafil, tadalafil and vardenafil in the last 24 to 48 hours. Patients do not volunteer it. Ask it plainly and privately. The combination drops the pressure to a level people do not walk away from.

Long-acting nitrates

Isosorbide mononitrate and dinitrate, plus nitroglycerin patches and ointment. These prevent chest pain rather than treat it.

The problem is tolerance. Around the clock dosing stops working within days.

So build in a nitrate-free interval of 10 to 12 hours each day. In practice that means the patch comes off at night and goes on in the morning. Rotate the site, remove the old patch first, and wear gloves with the ointment so you are not dosing yourself.

Bottom Line: Sit down, one tablet, 5 minutes, call 911, up to three while EMS comes. Amber bottle. Check a pressure. Ask about the PDE5 inhibitor before you hand over a nitrate.

Adenosine is a technique, not just a dose

The half-life is under 10 seconds. Push it slowly and it is gone before it reaches the heart.

Use the port closest to the patient. Push it as fast as you can, then slam a 20 mL flush behind it and raise the arm.

Warn the patient first. A brief pause on the monitor, flushing and a heavy chest are expected and they pass in seconds.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Fast Push, Big Flush
Adenosine is 6 mg then 12 mg, rapid push, 20 mL flush, closest port to the heart. Caffeine blocks the receptor and blunts the drug. Asthma is a caution, because it can trigger bronchospasm.
Amiodarone has a long tail

The half-life runs to weeks, so effects and interactions persist long after the drug is stopped.

Long-term monitoring is thyroid function, liver enzymes and lung findings. Teach sunscreen; photosensitivity is common. Warn about a blue-grey skin tint with prolonged use.

Atropine has one important failure. It speeds the AV node, so it works when the node is tired. In a block below the node there is nothing for it to speed up, and the answer is pacing.

07

Clot drugs

Discrimination axis: where the clot was made.

Students memorise two lists and then cannot pick between them. There is one question that sorts every clot drug, and it is about location.

Fast flow, arteries: antiplatelets

A plaque cracks in a coronary or a carotid. Platelets pile onto it. Blood is moving fast, so the clot is platelet-heavy.

Drugs: aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor, prasugrel.

Scenarios: chest pain, after an MI, after a stent, stroke prevention in plaque disease.

Slow flow, veins and a quivering atrium: anticoagulants

Blood pools in a leg vein or a fibrillating atrium. Slow blood clots through the cascade, so the clot is fibrin-heavy.

Drugs: heparin, enoxaparin, warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban.

Scenarios: A-fib, DVT, pulmonary embolism, mechanical valves, immobility.

The axis that separates them

Fast blood makes platelet clots. Slow blood makes fibrin clots. Antiplatelets for the first. Anticoagulants for the second.

So a stent gets aspirin and clopidogrel. Atrial fibrillation gets warfarin or a DOAC. Aspirin is not adequate stroke prevention in A-fib, and that swap is a favourite distractor.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Where Was the Clot Made
Ask where the clot formed before you pick a drug. Artery and plaque means antiplatelet. Vein or a fibrillating atrium means anticoagulant. Neither one dissolves a clot that already exists; both stop the next one.

A falling platelet count on heparin

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia usually appears 5 to 14 days after heparin starts.

Suspect it when platelets fall more than 50 percent from baseline, or below 150,000. Despite the low count, the patient clots rather than bleeds.

The action is not bleeding precautions and carry on. Stop all heparin, including flushes, and tell the provider.

Warfarin teaching, said properly

The most common wrong answer is telling a patient to avoid green vegetables. That is not the teaching.

Vitamin K is the reversal agent, so vitamin K foods work against the drug. The dose is titrated to whatever the patient normally eats. Sudden changes in either direction move the INR.

So the instruction is consistency, not avoidance. Eat about the same amount of spinach, kale and broccoli each week. Tell the provider before starting any new drug, including over the counter ones. No alcohol binges. Report black stools, pink urine, bruising and nosebleeds.

Enoxaparin technique

Deep subcutaneous, into the abdomen, at least two inches from the umbilicus. Do not expel the air bubble in a prefilled syringe. Do not aspirate. Do not rub the site afterwards.

The dentist scenario

A patient with a fresh stent is told by a dentist to hold the clopidogrel for a week.

The answer is always to call cardiology before anything is stopped. Stopping dual therapy early is how stents thrombose, and stent thrombosis kills a large share of the people it happens to.

Bleeding precautions are the same teaching for every drug in this section. Soft toothbrush. Electric razor. No contact sports. No aspirin or NSAIDs unless prescribed. Medical alert identification. Report black stools, pink or red urine, bruising, and any bleed that will not stop.

Statins and the lipid drugs

Statins block the enzyme that makes cholesterol in the liver. They lower LDL, and they thicken the cap on a plaque so it is less likely to rupture.

That second effect matters. It is why a patient already at goal LDL stays on one.

What to knowDetail
NamesAtorvastatin, rosuvastatin, simvastatin, pravastatin.
High intensityAtorvastatin 40 to 80 mg or rosuvastatin 20 to 40 mg. Used after an MI and in established coronary disease.
TimingSimvastatin and lovastatin go in the evening. Atorvastatin and rosuvastatin work at any hour.
MuscleNew muscle pain, tenderness or weakness gets reported the same day. Check a CK.
LiverBaseline liver enzymes, then as indicated. Report dark urine, yellow skin, right upper belly pain.
GrapefruitGrapefruit juice raises levels of several statins and with them the muscle risk.
PregnancyNot used in pregnancy. Ask about pregnancy plans before starting one.
Muscle pain is not a minor complaint

Most statin muscle aches are mild and settle. A small number are not.

Rhabdomyolysis breaks muscle down and the debris blocks the kidneys. The pattern is severe muscle pain and weakness with dark, tea-coloured urine.

That combination is a same-day call, not a wait-and-see. The risk climbs when a statin is paired with a fibrate.

09

What you do first

Medication questions hand you four defensible actions and ask which comes first. All four are things a nurse does. That is the trick. You are being tested on ordering.

The ordering rules
  • Check the parameter before the dose. Pulse, pressure or lab first, drug second. Assessment beats administration.
  • Hold and notify. Never skip a dose silently. A held dose that nobody hears about is an error.
  • Potassium before digoxin. If the level is low, the level of digoxin does not tell you the truth.
  • Pressure before anything that lowers it. Nitroglycerin, morphine, beta-blockers and calcium channel blockers all drop it.
  • Never stop a beta-blocker abruptly. Not for a low pressure, not before surgery, not because the patient feels fine.
  • Least invasive first. Position and rest before drugs. Vagal maneuvers before adenosine, adenosine before electricity.

Two habits that prevent most drug errors here

Take the apical pulse for a full minute in any patient on digoxin or a beta-blocker. A 15 second count multiplied by four misses an irregular rhythm.

Treat heparin as the high-alert drug it is. Independent double check on the pump settings and the concentration, every bag.

Bottom Line: The correct first action is almost always the assessment that gates the drug. Take the pulse. Read the potassium. Check the pressure. Then decide about the dose.

Key Takeaways

Beta-blockers and digoxin = apical pulse for a full minute, hold if under 60

Loops and thiazides lower K+; ACE, ARB, ARNI and aldosterone antagonists raise it

The ACE cough is an expected effect; the fix is a switch to an ARB

Nitroglycerin = sit down, one tablet, 5 minutes, call 911, up to 3 total

Heparin aPTT 1.5-2.5x control, protamine; warfarin INR 2-3, vitamin K

Statins = report new muscle pain, especially with dark urine
