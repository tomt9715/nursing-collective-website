Documentation and Charting

One rule underneath all of it: write what you saw, not what you concluded. Everything else in this guide is that rule wearing different clothes.

By the end, without notes

  • Rewrite a judgmental entry as an objective one, in one pass
  • Correct a written error the legal way, and say why the other ways fail
  • Take a telephone order safely, and say when it must be signed
  • Give an SBAR handoff without notes

01

What actually gets you

Charting feels like paperwork. It is the only proof that the care happened.

Points go in four places, and they are the same four every time.

You charted your conclusion instead of your observation

Uncooperative. Confused. Doing well. Appears anxious.

Every one of those is a judgment. Write what produced it. Pulled out the intravenous line twice. Could not state the year. Walked the corridor without stopping.

You fixed a mistake by hiding it

Erasing, scribbling out, correction fluid and deleting all look like concealment later.

A single line through the entry, with your initials, the date and the time, is the only acceptable way. The original must still be readable.

You charted it before you did it

Pre-charting saves a few minutes and it is falsification.

The patient refuses the drug, or you get pulled to an emergency, and the record now says something that never happened. Chart after the care, as close to it as you can.

You put the incident report in the chart

The chart records what happened to the patient. The incident report is an internal document about the system.

Writing incident report completed in the note pulls that document into the legal record. Chart the facts, file the report, and never link them.

Bottom Line: Observation not conclusion, correction not concealment, after not before, and the two documents stay apart.
02

The four things

Four documents carry this topic. For each one you should be able to say four things with the book closed.

What it is. What belongs in it. What never does. And the rule that gets tested.

Document What it is What belongs What never belongs The rule
The nursing note The clinical and legal record of this patient. What you observed, what you did, how they responded, who you notified. Opinions, blame, staffing complaints, other patients' names. Objective, timely, and signed by the person who did it.
The order A prescriber's instruction, sometimes taken by phone. Date, time, full order, prescriber name, read back and verified. Anything unclear, and anything you would not carry out. Write it down, read it back, get it signed.
The handoff Transfer of responsibility to the next nurse or unit. Situation, background, assessment, recommendation, and time to ask questions. Guesswork, gossip, and anything you have not verified. Structured, two-way, and at the bedside where possible.
The incident report An internal quality document about a system failure. What happened, when, who was involved, what was done. Any mention of it in the patient's chart. File it separately, and never reference it in the record.
Copy this down

Four rows on one index card, by hand. Then cover the last two columns and rebuild them. Those two columns are what the questions are made of.

03

Objective or subjective

Discrimination axis: could another nurse have seen or heard exactly this.

If yes, it is objective and it belongs. If it required your interpretation, rewrite it.

Do not writeWrite instead
Patient is uncooperativeRefused morning medications and declined to be repositioned at 0800 and 1000
Patient is confusedUnable to state name, year or location. Oriented to person only
Patient appears anxiousReports feeling on edge. Pacing, hands trembling, heart rate 108
Tolerated the procedure wellRemained still, heart rate 78 to 84 throughout, denied pain afterwards
Ate a good breakfastAte approximately 75 percent of the breakfast tray
Large amount of drainageDrainage of 150 mL, serosanguineous, no odour
Patient seems to be in painGrimacing on movement, guarding the right flank, rates pain 7 out of 10
Family is difficultDaughter asked to speak with the provider about the plan of care
Subjective data still belongs

Subjective is not banned. What the patient says is important data, and it goes in quotation marks as they said it.

Patient states, my chest feels like someone is sitting on it. That is a direct quote and it is stronger evidence than any summary of it.

What you exclude is your interpretation offered as fact.

The qualities of a defensible entry
  • Factual. Observed and measured, not inferred
  • Accurate. Exact numbers, exact times, exact quotes
  • Complete. What you found, what you did, who you told, how they responded
  • Current. Written as soon after the care as possible
  • Organised. In the order it happened
  • Confidential. This patient only, never another patient's name

The most common gap is the response. You gave the drug, and the record never says whether it worked.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Write the Evidence, Not the Verdict
Ask what made you think that, and chart the answer instead. Uncooperative becomes refused twice. Confused becomes could not state the year. If a question asks which entry is appropriate, look for the one with numbers, quotes and observable behaviour.
Bottom Line: Another nurse should be able to picture the patient from your note without agreeing to any of your opinions.
04

Errors, late entries and blanks

Discrimination axis: is the original still readable.

That is what separates a correction from a cover-up, and it is what a court looks at.

Correcting a paper entry
  • One single line through the error
  • The original stays legible underneath
  • Write the correct information beside it
  • Initial, date and time the correction
  • Some facilities ask for the word error or a code beside it

Never: erase, scribble out, white out, or write over.

Correcting an electronic entry
  • Use the system's own amend or addendum function
  • The original entry stays visible in the audit trail
  • The correction is stamped with your name and the time
  • Never chart under another person's login to fix something

Never: delete. The record keeps a trace, and deletion reads as concealment.

Late entries

Care that was given and not charted still gets charted. It is labelled honestly.

Write late entry, give the date and time you are writing it, and state the date and time the care actually happened. Two timestamps, both true.

Never squeeze a forgotten entry into a gap between earlier notes. That is falsification.

The other paper rules
  • No blank lines. Draw a line through any unused space so nothing can be added later
  • Black ink, legible, and every entry signed with your name and credentials
  • Chart only care you performed. Never chart for another nurse
  • Never sign a note someone else wrote
  • Do not chart in advance, at any interval, for any reason
  • If care was omitted, chart that it was and why, plus who was notified
FlorenceBot Pro Tip
The Original Has to Stay Readable
One line, initials, date, time. Any option involving correction fluid, erasing, heavy scribbling or deleting is wrong, however tidy it sounds. The same logic explains why blank lines get struck through and why late entries carry two timestamps.
Bottom Line: Single line, initials, time. Late entries carry both times. Nothing is ever removed, only added to.
05

Abbreviations that are banned

These are not style preferences. Each one has hurt someone.

Do not useWhyWrite
URead as a zero or a four, so 6U becomes 60 or 64unit
IURead as IV, or as the number teninternational unit
QDThe tail of the Q looks like an I, so daily becomes four times a daydaily
QODConfused with QD and with QIDevery other day
Trailing zero, as in 1.0 mgA faint decimal point turns it into 10 mg1 mg
Missing leading zero, as in .5 mgRead as 5 mg, a tenfold overdose0.5 mg
MS, MSO4, MgSO4Morphine and magnesium confused with each otherthe full drug name
Also avoided
  • Drug name abbreviations such as HCT, MTX and AZT, which map to several drugs
  • Apothecary units, and the symbol for micrograms, which is read as milligrams
  • The greater than and less than symbols, which are read as each other
  • cc for millilitres, which is read as units when handwritten

An order containing one of these is clarified with the prescriber, never interpreted.

Bottom Line: Always a leading zero, never a trailing one, spell out unit and daily, and write drug names in full.
06

Charting formats

Facilities differ. The format changes the shape of the note, not the rules inside it.

FormatHow it is builtStrength and weakness
NarrativeChronological story of the shiftSimple and flexible. Long, repetitive, and hard to find one detail in
SOAPSubjective, objective, assessment, plan. SOAPIE adds intervention and evaluationOrganised around problems. Can fragment a patient into a list
PIEProblem, intervention, evaluationTies care to outcomes. Weak on background
Focus or DARData, action, response, focused on a concern rather than a problemReads well and includes the patient's own words. Needs discipline to stay focused
Charting by exceptionOnly findings outside a defined normal are writtenFast, and it makes abnormal findings jump out. Risky if the normal standard is not clearly defined
The electronic record
  • Your login is your signature. Never share it and never borrow one
  • Log off before you walk away, even for a moment
  • Position screens away from corridors and waiting areas
  • Copy and paste carries yesterday's findings into today. Verify before you accept anything
  • Every access is logged, including the ones nobody sees you make
  • Know the downtime procedure. Paper forms exist for when the system is unavailable, and they are entered afterwards
Bottom Line: Pick the format your facility uses. The objective rule, the timing rule and the correction rule do not change with it.
07

Handoff and SBAR

Most serious errors trace back to a handoff. Structure is what protects it.

SBAR, and what goes in each letter
  • Situation. Who you are, who the patient is, and what is happening right now
  • Background. Diagnosis, relevant history, what has been done so far
  • Assessment. Your findings and what you think is going on
  • Recommendation. What you are asking for, specifically

The recommendation is the letter students drop. Say what you want. I need you to come and see her, or I am requesting an order for a chest x-ray.

What a good shift handoff includes
  • Identity, diagnosis and code status
  • Current status and anything that changed this shift
  • Lines, drains, wounds, drips and devices
  • Pending results, pending procedures, and what still needs doing
  • Allergies, isolation status, fall or safety risk
  • Family situation and any communication in progress
  • Time for the incoming nurse to ask questions and read back

Bedside handoff involves the patient, catches errors the paper missed, and lets you both see the drip and the dressing.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Say What You Are Asking For
The recommendation is what turns a report into a request. Reporting the numbers and waiting to be told what to do is the version that fails. If a question asks what is missing from an SBAR call, check for the ask before you check anything else.
Bottom Line: Structured, two-way, at the bedside, and it ends with a specific request.
08

Telephone and verbal orders

Discrimination axis: was it written down and read back.

Verbal orders are limited to emergencies and to situations where the prescriber cannot enter the order themselves.

  1. 1
    Write it down as it is given. Never take an order from memory and enter it afterwards.
  2. 2
    Read it back word for word, including the drug, the dose, the route, the frequency and the spelling of anything that sounds like another drug.
  3. 3
    Get verbal confirmation from the prescriber that the read-back is correct.
  4. 4
    Record it fully. Date, time, the complete order, the prescriber's name, then your name and the note that it was read back and verified.
  5. 5
    Get it countersigned, commonly within 24 hours, according to your facility policy.
The same rule for critical results

A critical laboratory value received by phone is written down and read back the same way.

Then it is reported to the provider promptly, and the chart records the value, the time you were told, the time you notified, and what was ordered.

Consent, and what you are documenting

The provider explains the procedure, the risks, the benefits and the alternatives. That is not a nursing task.

You witness the signature, confirm the patient is competent and not premedicated, and document that. If the patient cannot describe what they agreed to, stop and call the provider back.

Bottom Line: Write, read back, verify, record, sign within the window. Same sequence for critical results.
09

Privacy, and how it gets breached

Almost no breach involves malice. They happen through convenience.

The ones that happen on real units
  • Discussing a patient in an elevator, a corridor or a cafeteria
  • Looking up the record of a friend, a relative, a colleague or yourself
  • Leaving a screen unlocked, or a printed report on a counter
  • Posting anything about a patient online, even with the name removed
  • Photographing a wound on a personal phone
  • Telling a family member something the patient has not authorised

Access is logged. Curiosity about a record you are not caring for is a breach on its own.

The principle underneath

Minimum necessary. You access what you need to give care, and nothing beyond it.

Information is shared with the team caring for the patient. Everyone else, including family, needs the patient's permission.

The record belongs to the facility. The information in it belongs to the patient, and they have the right to see it.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Caring About Someone Is Not Authorisation
Looking up a colleague, a neighbour or your own record is a breach even when the motive is kind. So is a social media post with the name removed, because the details still identify the person. If you are not caring for them, you do not open the chart.
Bottom Line: Minimum necessary, care team only, screens locked, and nothing about a patient goes online.
10

What you do first

Documentation questions are usually ordering questions in disguise.

The ordering rules
  • The patient comes before the paperwork. Assess and act, then chart
  • Clarify an unclear order before you carry it out
  • Chart as soon after the care as possible, and never before
  • Report a critical value before you document it
  • When an entry is wrong, correct it openly rather than tidily
  • When you are unsure whether something belongs in the chart, ask whether it is about this patient's care
Answers that are almost always wrong
  • Using correction fluid, erasing or deleting an entry
  • Charting care before it is given
  • Charting for another nurse, or under another person's login
  • Documenting that an incident report was completed
  • Recording an opinion about a patient or a family
  • Leaving blank lines in a paper record
  • Accepting a verbal order without writing it down and reading it back
  • Opening the record of someone you are not caring for

Learn this list. It eliminates two options in most questions on this topic.

Bottom Line: Patient first, then the record. Openly, promptly, objectively, and only about the person in front of you.
