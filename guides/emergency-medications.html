Emergency Medications

01

What actually gets you

Emergency drugs are a short list. That is the good news.

The bad news is that the questions are never really about the drug. They are about sequence.

You reached for a drug when the answer was electricity

In a shockable rhythm, defibrillation is the treatment. Drugs are what you give around it.

So a pulseless patient in ventricular fibrillation gets a shock, not a syringe. Epinephrine and amiodarone matter, and neither converts the rhythm.

You gave the right drug the wrong way

Several of these drugs fail entirely on technique.

Adenosine given slowly does nothing, because its half-life is seconds. Epinephrine for anaphylaxis given subcutaneously absorbs too slowly. Concentrated potassium given by push kills.

Route and speed are part of the dose here, not a detail underneath it.

You treated the number instead of the membrane

In hyperkalemia with electrocardiogram changes, the first drug is calcium.

Calcium does not lower the potassium at all. It stabilises the heart so the patient survives long enough for the drugs that do.

Missing that ordering is the single most common error in this topic.

You forgot the antidote wears off

Naloxone lasts 30 to 90 minutes. Plenty of opioids last far longer.

So the patient who woke up is not finished. They are on a clock, and they can sedate again once the reversal fades.

Bottom Line: Rhythm first, then technique, then ordering. Most wrong answers here are the right drug at the wrong moment.
02

The four things

Five groups carry this topic. For each one you should be able to say four things with the book closed.

What it is for. The dose. How it is given. And the thing that makes it fail.

Group What it is for The headline dose How it is given What makes it fail
Arrest drugs Cardiac arrest, any rhythm. Epinephrine 1 mg. Amiodarone 300 mg. IV or intraosseous push, followed by a flush and limb elevation. Giving them instead of compressions and defibrillation.
Rhythm drugs Bradycardia and tachycardia with a pulse. Atropine 1 mg. Adenosine 6 mg, then 12 mg. Atropine every 3 to 5 minutes. Adenosine as fast as you can, with a 20 mL flush. Pushing adenosine slowly, or giving atropine below 1 mg.
Vasopressors Shock that persists after fluid. Titrated in micrograms per kilogram per minute, or per minute. Infusion on a pump, ideally through a central line. Running one through a poor peripheral line and missing the extravasation.
Antidotes Reversing a specific drug or toxin. Specific to each one. Mostly IV, and mostly repeated. Assuming one dose finished the job.
Anaphylaxis Airway swelling or hypotension after an exposure. Epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg of 1:1,000, to 0.5 mg in an adult. Intramuscular, into the vastus lateralis, repeated every 5 to 15 minutes. Giving an antihistamine first, or giving it subcutaneously.

03

Cardiac arrest

Discrimination axis: is this rhythm shockable.

That question decides the entire algorithm. Ask it before you think about any drug.

Shockable

Rhythms: ventricular fibrillation, and pulseless ventricular tachycardia.

  • Defibrillate immediately, then resume compressions at once
  • Epinephrine 1 mg every 3 to 5 minutes
  • Amiodarone 300 mg after shocks have failed, with a second dose of 150 mg if needed
  • Lidocaine is the alternative when amiodarone is unavailable

The point: electricity converts the rhythm. The drugs support it.

Not shockable

Rhythms: asystole, and pulseless electrical activity.

  • Do not shock. There is no organised electrical activity to reset
  • High-quality compressions, and epinephrine 1 mg every 3 to 5 minutes
  • Everything else is finding and fixing the cause

The point: in these rhythms the reversible cause is the treatment.

The axis that separates them

Chaotic or fast organised electrical activity can be reset by a shock. A flat line or an electrical pattern with no mechanical output cannot.

So the answer for asystole is never defibrillation, however dramatic that would be. It is compressions, epinephrine, and a hunt for the cause.

The reversible causes

Pulseless electrical activity in particular is a question about cause, and the list is worth holding.

  • Low volume, low oxygen, acidosis
  • Potassium too high or too low
  • Low body temperature, and low glucose
  • Tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade
  • Toxins, including opioids and beta blockers
  • Clot in the lung, and clot in the coronary artery

When a stem hands you a dialysis patient in arrest, potassium is the answer waiting to be found.

How a drug reaches the heart during an arrest

Circulation during compressions is poor, so administration technique matters more than usual.

  • Push the drug rapidly into the line closest to the heart
  • Follow with a 20 mL flush
  • Raise the limb for 10 to 20 seconds
  • Intraosseous access is equivalent when a vein cannot be found
  • Compressions continue through all of it
FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Sort the Rhythm Before You Pick a Drug
Say shockable or not shockable out loud from the stem, then read the options. Ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia get electricity first. Asystole and pulseless electrical activity never do, and any option offering a shock for asystole is wrong.
Bottom Line: Shockable gets a shock, then epinephrine, then amiodarone. Not shockable gets compressions, epinephrine and a cause.
04

Too slow, or too fast, with a pulse

Discrimination axis: is the patient stable.

Stable buys you time and a drug. Unstable means electricity now.

Unstable looks like hypotension, altered consciousness, chest pain, or signs of shock.

DrugUsed forDose and techniqueWhat you watch
Atropine Symptomatic bradycardia 1 mg IV, repeated every 3 to 5 minutes, to a maximum of 3 mg Doses under 1 mg can slow the heart further, which is why the first dose is a full milligram. Dry mouth and urinary retention follow
Adenosine Stable narrow-complex tachycardia, after vagal maneuvers 6 mg by rapid push over 1 to 2 seconds, then a 20 mL flush. A second dose of 12 mg if needed The half-life is under 10 seconds. Expect a brief pause on the monitor, and warn the patient it feels alarming
Amiodarone Ventricular arrhythmias, and rate control 300 mg IV in arrest. An infusion when there is a pulse Hypotension and bradycardia. Long term, the thyroid, liver and lungs
Magnesium sulfate Torsades de pointes 1 to 2 g IV Hypotension. Check reflexes on a continuous infusion, because losing them signals toxicity
Defibrillation or cardioversion

Two different things, and the difference is timing.

  • Defibrillation is unsynchronised. It is for pulseless ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia
  • Synchronised cardioversion times the shock to the R wave. It is for unstable tachycardia with a pulse

Delivering an unsynchronised shock to a patient with a pulse can land it on the T wave and produce ventricular fibrillation. That is why the synchronise button exists.

Sedate a conscious patient before a synchronised cardioversion when time allows.

Adenosine technique, which is the tested part
  • Use the port closest to the patient, and the largest vein available
  • Push over 1 to 2 seconds. A slow push is a wasted dose
  • Follow immediately with 20 mL of saline, ideally with a second person or a stopcock
  • Have the monitor running and a strip recording
  • Tell the patient they may feel chest tightness, flushing and a sense of doom for a few seconds

That brief asystolic pause on the monitor is expected. It is the drug working, not the patient arresting.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Slow Adenosine Is No Adenosine
Its half-life is under 10 seconds, so a careful push is the same as not giving it. Any answer describing a slow or diluted adenosine push is wrong. The same logic runs the other way for concentrated potassium, where a push is lethal and slow is the only safe answer.
Bottom Line: Stable gets a drug, unstable gets electricity. Atropine starts at a full 1 mg, adenosine goes fast, and cardioversion is synchronised.
05

Vasopressors and inotropes

Discrimination axis: is the problem an empty tank, a weak pump, or loose pipes.

Pressors do not fix volume. Fluid comes first unless the heart is the problem.

DrugWhat it mainly doesTypical use
NorepinephrineTightens the vessels, with some increase in contractilityFirst choice in septic shock once fluid has been given
EpinephrineRaises rate, contractility and vessel toneCardiac arrest, anaphylaxis, and shock not responding to other agents
DopamineEffects change with the rate, from renal to inotropic to vasoconstrictingLess used now. Causes more arrhythmia than norepinephrine
DobutamineIncreases contractility and can drop the pressureCardiogenic shock and decompensated heart failure. It is an inotrope, not a pressor
VasopressinTightens vessels through a different receptorAdded to norepinephrine to reduce the dose needed
Extravasation is the emergency you will actually see

A vasopressor that leaks into tissue constricts the vessels there and the tissue dies.

Signs are pallor or blanching around the site, coolness, swelling, pain, and a slowing infusion.

  • Stop the infusion and leave the catheter in place
  • Aspirate what you can through the catheter, then remove it as directed
  • Notify immediately and elevate the limb
  • Expect phentolamine to be prescribed, infiltrated locally to reverse the constriction
  • Mark the area, document it, and keep reassessing

Central access is preferred for exactly this reason. Peripheral use happens, and it is watched closely.

Running one safely
  • Always on a pump, never by gravity, and never as a bolus
  • Continuous cardiac monitoring and frequent pressure checks, often by arterial line
  • Titrate to the ordered target, commonly a mean arterial pressure, rather than to a feeling
  • Never stop one abruptly. The pressure falls with it
  • Independent double check, because these are high-alert drugs
  • Check the site at every assessment, and more often peripherally
FlorenceBot Pro Tip
A Pressor Cannot Squeeze an Empty Tank
In hypovolemic and septic shock, fluid comes before the pressor, and a stem that skips straight to norepinephrine in an obviously dry patient is testing that. The exception is cardiogenic shock, where the pump has failed and more fluid makes it worse.
Bottom Line: Fluid first, then a pressor on a pump, titrated to a target. Blanching around the site means stop and call.
06

The antidote table

This is a memorisation section, and it is worth memorising cleanly.

ProblemAntidoteThe thing that gets tested
Opioid overdoseNaloxoneIt lasts 30 to 90 minutes, shorter than most opioids, so resedation is expected. It also reverses analgesia and can precipitate withdrawal
Benzodiazepine overdoseFlumazenilNot routine. It can precipitate seizures in dependence or a mixed overdose, so supportive care is the mainstay
Acetaminophen overdoseAcetylcysteineWorks best given early. Time since ingestion drives everything
Warfarin, high INR or bleedingVitamin KSlow to work, so active bleeding also needs factor replacement or plasma
Heparin overdoseProtamine sulfateReverses unfractionated heparin well, low molecular weight heparin only partly
Digoxin toxicityDigoxin immune fabUsed for life-threatening toxicity. Check the potassium, because it moves in both directions
Magnesium toxicityCalcium gluconateLost deep tendon reflexes are the early warning, before respiratory depression
Beta blocker overdoseGlucagonIt raises heart rate and contractility through a pathway the blockade does not touch
Hypoglycemia, unable to swallowDextrose 50% IV, or glucagonGlucagon needs liver glycogen, so it can fail in starvation or alcohol use
Organophosphate poisoningAtropine, with pralidoximeDrying the secretions is the goal, and large repeated doses may be needed
Every antidote has a clock

The common thread across the table is duration.

Naloxone fades before the opioid does. Vitamin K takes hours. Acetylcysteine loses effect with delay. Digoxin immune fab shifts potassium as it works.

So giving the antidote is the beginning of the nursing care, not the end of it. Keep monitoring, and expect repeat dosing.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
The Patient Who Woke Up Is Still a Patient
After naloxone, the correct answer is almost never to relax the monitoring. It is to keep watching, because the opioid outlasts the reversal. Any option that discharges, transfers or stops observing a reversed patient shortly after they wake is wrong.
Bottom Line: Ten pairs, and one shared rule. The antidote starts the care rather than finishing it.
07

Anaphylaxis

Discrimination axis: is the airway or the blood pressure involved.

Skin-only reactions are uncomfortable. Airway or pressure makes it anaphylaxis, and the drug changes.

Epinephrine, first, every time
  • Dose: 0.01 mg/kg of 1:1,000, up to 0.3 mg in a child and 0.5 mg in an adolescent or adult
  • Route: intramuscular, into the vastus lateralis, the mid-anterolateral thigh
  • Repeat: every 5 to 15 minutes if symptoms persist
  • Auto-injectors: 0.15 mg below 30 kg, 0.3 mg at 30 kg and above

Not subcutaneous, and not the deltoid. The thigh absorbs fastest, and delay is the strongest predictor of death.

Everything else is an adjunct
  • Remove the trigger. Stop the infusion, remove the stinger
  • Airway and oxygen. Be ready to secure it, because swelling progresses
  • Position supine with the legs raised, unless breathing is easier sitting up
  • Fluid. A normal saline bolus of 20 mL/kg for hypotension
  • Antihistamines and steroids. They help the skin and the later phase. Neither opens an airway

An answer offering diphenhydramine before epinephrine is always wrong.

It can come back

Symptoms can return hours after they settle, usually within 8 to 10 hours and occasionally much later.

That biphasic reaction is why patients are observed rather than discharged once they look well, and why they go home with two auto-injectors.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
The Antihistamine Is a Decoy
When a stem gives you wheeze, throat tightness or a falling pressure after an exposure, the first action is intramuscular epinephrine in the thigh. Diphenhydramine and steroids appear in the options to see whether you will reach for the gentler drug. Do not.
Bottom Line: Airway or pressure means epinephrine, intramuscular, in the thigh, now. Everything else supports it.
08

Three more emergencies with a drug answer

Hyperkalemia

Discrimination axis: protecting the heart, or lowering the potassium.

Those are two different jobs, done by two different sets of drugs, in a fixed order.

StepDrugWhat it actually does
1. ProtectCalcium gluconate or calcium chlorideStabilises the cardiac membrane. It does not lower the potassium at all. Given first when there are electrocardiogram changes
2. ShiftInsulin with dextrose, and a nebulised beta agonist. Bicarbonate if acidoticDrives potassium into the cells. Fast, and temporary. Watch the glucose
3. RemovePotassium binders, diuretics, dialysisThe only step that takes potassium out of the body. Slowest, and definitive
Why calcium comes first

The patient does not die of the number. They die of the arrhythmia the number causes.

Calcium raises the threshold the heart needs to misfire, buying time for the shifting and removing drugs to work. Skipping it to go straight to insulin is the classic wrong sequence.

Peaked T waves are the early electrocardiogram change, and a widening QRS is the sign that time is short.

Hypoglycemia

Can they swallow safely

That question picks the route, and it is the whole decision.

  • Awake and able to swallow: 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate, recheck in 15 minutes, repeat if still low
  • Unable to swallow, with IV access: dextrose 50% intravenously
  • Unable to swallow, no IV access: glucagon intramuscularly or subcutaneously
  • Follow with a complex carbohydrate and protein once they are able, or the level falls again

Glucagon works by releasing stored glycogen, so it can fail in starvation, liver disease or heavy alcohol use.

Status epilepticus

Benzodiazepine first, then a longer agent

A seizure that will not stop is treated in two stages, and the order is fixed.

  • First: a benzodiazepine such as lorazepam, midazolam or diazepam, to abort the seizure
  • Then: a longer-acting anticonvulsant such as levetiracetam, fosphenytoin or valproate, to keep it stopped
  • Protect the airway, give oxygen, check the glucose, and do not restrain the limbs

Giving only the benzodiazepine leaves the patient likely to seize again once it wears off.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Protect, Shift, Remove
Three words in that order answer almost every hyperkalemia question. Calcium protects and changes no number. Insulin and albuterol shift and are temporary. Only dialysis, binders and diuretics remove. A question that asks what you give first with peaked T waves is asking for calcium.
Bottom Line: Calcium protects before anything shifts. Swallowing decides the glucose route. A seizure needs a benzodiazepine and then something longer.
09

Giving them safely, at speed

Emergency drugs are high-alert drugs given in the worst conditions for accuracy.

The rules that do not bend for an emergency
  • Concentrated potassium is never pushed. Always diluted, always on a pump
  • Read the concentration, not just the drug name. Epinephrine comes as 1:1,000 and as 1:10,000
  • Closed-loop communication. Repeat the order back, state the drug and dose as you give it, and say when it is in
  • One person records, with times, so the 3 to 5 minute intervals are real rather than estimated
  • Independent double check where the situation allows it
  • Keep the empty vials until the code is documented
The two epinephrine concentrations

This is the most dangerous look-alike in the topic, and it is worth holding clearly.

  • 1:1,000 is 1 mg per mL. Small volumes, intramuscular, for anaphylaxis
  • 1:10,000 is 1 mg per 10 mL. The prefilled syringe, intravenous, for cardiac arrest

The dose in cardiac arrest is the same 1 mg. The volume and the route are what differ, and grabbing the wrong one gives a tenfold error.

The cart itself
  • Checked on a schedule, and the lock intact means it is complete
  • Broken lock means restock and re-check before it is trusted
  • Defibrillator tested at every check, with pads in date
  • Know where yours is before your first shift, not during your first code
Bottom Line: Concentration, route and speed are part of the dose. Say the drug out loud, and record the time.
10

What you do first

Every option is plausible. Ordering is the whole question.

The ordering rules
  • No pulse means compressions and a rhythm check before any drug
  • A shockable rhythm gets the shock before the syringe
  • Unstable with a pulse means electricity, not a drug
  • Airway compromise outranks the drug that will eventually fix the cause
  • In hyperkalemia with electrocardiogram changes, calcium comes before anything else
  • Fluid before a pressor, unless the pump is the problem
  • After any reversal agent, keep monitoring rather than stepping back
Answers that are almost always wrong
  • Defibrillating asystole
  • Pushing adenosine slowly, or without a rapid flush
  • Giving atropine at less than 1 mg for symptomatic bradycardia
  • Diphenhydramine before epinephrine in anaphylaxis
  • Epinephrine subcutaneously for anaphylaxis, or into the deltoid
  • Insulin before calcium in hyperkalemia with electrocardiogram changes
  • Pushing concentrated potassium, at any speed
  • Reducing monitoring after naloxone because the patient woke up
  • Starting a pressor in an obviously hypovolemic patient before fluid

Learn this list. It eliminates two options in most questions on this topic.

Bottom Line: Pulse, rhythm, stability, then the drug. When two options are both correct actions, the one that keeps the heart beating goes first.
