Infection Control

One question decides almost every answer here: how does this organism travel. Get that right and the room, the mask and the gloves fall out of it.

01

What actually gets you

Infection control looks like memorisation. It is not.

It is one question asked over and over. How does this organism get from that patient to the next one.

Points go in four predictable places.

You learned lists of diseases instead of routes

Nobody can hold every organism in memory. You do not have to.

Learn the route, then attach a handful of diseases to it. A stem that names an organism you have never heard of still tells you how it spreads, and that is the answer.

You reached for the alcohol rub every time

Alcohol is faster and kinder to your skin, so it became the reflex.

It does not kill spores. Two situations override it: visibly soiled hands, and a patient with Clostridioides difficile. Both need soap, water and friction.

You memorised the donning order and skipped the doffing order

Putting equipment on is the easy half. Almost nobody self-contaminates on the way in.

They do it on the way out. The order for removal exists because gloves are the dirtiest thing you are wearing, so gloves come off first, and hand hygiene follows.

You forgot which way the air is meant to move

Two rooms look identical on paper. They do opposite jobs.

Negative pressure keeps the organism in, for a patient who is infectious. Positive pressure keeps everything out, for a patient who cannot fight infection. Put a tuberculosis patient in the wrong one and you have ventilated the unit.

Bottom Line: Route first, then room, then equipment. Every precaution in this guide is downstream of how the organism travels.
02

The four things

Four precaution levels carry this topic. For each one you should be able to say four things with the book closed.

What it is. Who gets it. What you wear. And the room it needs.

If you cannot say all four out loud, you do not know it yet.

Level What it is Who gets it What you wear Room
Standard The floor. Assumes every patient's blood and body fluid is infectious. Every patient, every time, diagnosis or not. Hand hygiene always. Gloves, gown, mask and eye protection whenever a splash is possible. Any room.
Contact Added when the organism travels by touch, on hands and surfaces. MRSA, VRE, C. difficile, scabies, lice, norovirus, RSV, draining wounds. Gown and gloves, on before you enter. Private room, or cohort with the same organism. Dedicated equipment.
Droplet Added for large droplets that fall out of the air within a few feet. Influenza, pertussis, mumps, rubella, meningococcal disease, group A strep. Surgical mask on entry, plus gloves and gown for contact with fluids. Private room, door may stay open. Mask the patient for transport.
Airborne Added for tiny particles that stay suspended and drift. Tuberculosis, measles, varicella, disseminated zoster. Fit-tested N95 respirator or higher, put on before you enter. Negative-pressure room, door closed. Mask the patient for transport.
Copy this down

Four rows on one index card, by hand. Standard is everyone. Contact is gown and gloves. Droplet is a surgical mask. Airborne is an N95 and negative pressure. That card answers most of the questions on this topic.

03

The chain, and where you break it

Discrimination axis: which link is this question actually about.

Infection needs six things in a row. Take out any one and it stops.

Questions rarely say chain of infection. They hand you an action and ask what it prevents.

LinkWhat it meansHow you break it
Infectious agentThe organism itselfCleaning, disinfection, sterilisation, treating the infection
ReservoirWhere it lives and multiplies: people, water, equipment, foodChange the wet dressing, empty the drainage bag, clean the shared machine
Portal of exitHow it leaves the host: cough, wound, stool, bloodCover the cough, contain the drainage, handle linen away from your uniform
Mode of transmissionContact, droplet, airborne, vehicle, vectorHand hygiene. This is the link you break most, and the one questions target
Portal of entryHow it gets into the next host: a line, a catheter, broken skin, the airwaySterile technique, catheter and line care, intact skin, oral care
Susceptible hostSomeone whose defences are downVaccination, nutrition, glucose control, protective precautions
The link they mean

When a question asks which action best prevents transmission, and hand hygiene is an option, hand hygiene is the answer.

It is not a trick. It is the single most effective action, and mode of transmission is the link nurses control on every patient, all shift.

Who counts as a susceptible host

Some patients are on the wrong side of this chain before you walk in.

The risk stack
  • Very young and very old, where the immune response is immature or slowed
  • Chemotherapy, steroids, transplant drugs, biologics
  • Poorly controlled diabetes, malnutrition, obesity
  • Any breach in the skin: surgical wound, burn, pressure injury, intravenous line
  • Every invasive device, and every extra day it stays in
  • Recent antibiotics, which clear the organisms that were holding the space

Stack two or more and the answer shifts toward prevention rather than treatment.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Name the Link, Then Answer
When a stem describes an action and asks what it prevents, name the link before you read the options. Emptying a urine bag is reservoir. Wearing a mask is transmission. Turning a patient is portal of entry. Naming the link first stops you picking the option that is true but is about a different link.
Bottom Line: Six links, and you break the fourth one dozens of times a shift. Hand hygiene is not a courtesy. It is the intervention.
04

Hand hygiene, and when alcohol fails

Discrimination axis: is there a spore, or is there visible soil.

Alcohol rub is the default in most situations. It works faster and it is easier on your skin.

Two things defeat it. Both show up in questions constantly.

Alcohol-based rub
  • Routine hand hygiene, before and after almost every contact
  • Cover every surface, including thumbs, fingertips and between fingers
  • Rub until completely dry, which takes about 20 seconds
  • Faster and less drying than soap, so compliance is higher

Fails against: spores, and anything you can see on your hands.

Soap and water
  • Hands visibly soiled with blood, body fluid, dirt or powder
  • Care of a patient with C. difficile, every time, no exceptions
  • Suspected exposure to anthrax, another spore former
  • After using the toilet, and before eating

How: lather all surfaces at least 20 seconds. Friction is what removes spores, not the soap.

The axis that separates them

Spores and visible soil. That is the whole rule.

Alcohol destroys the membranes of bacteria and most viruses. A spore has a coat that alcohol cannot get through, so the only way to deal with it is to physically wash it off and send it down the drain.

Norovirus is the third name worth knowing here. Alcohol is unreliable against it, so soap and water is preferred during an outbreak.

The five moments

When, not just how
  • Before touching the patient
  • Before a clean or aseptic procedure
  • After exposure to body fluid, or after removing gloves
  • After touching the patient
  • After touching anything in the patient's surroundings, even if you never touched the patient

The last one is the one people skip. Touching a pump, a rail or a chart and walking out counts.

Gloves are not hand hygiene

Gloves leak, and hands get contaminated during removal. That is measurable, and it is why the rule exists.

Hand hygiene happens before gloves go on and immediately after they come off. Every pair, every patient.

Wearing the same gloves from one patient to another is worse than wearing none, because it feels safe.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Spores Do Not Care About Alcohol
If the stem says C. difficile, three things change at once: soap and water rather than rub, a bleach-based disinfectant rather than the usual wipe, and contact precautions with dedicated equipment. Any answer offering alcohol rub for this patient is wrong, however well it is worded.
Bottom Line: Rub for most, wash for spores and soil. Twenty seconds either way, and gloves never replace it.
05

Sorting the organism into a route

Discrimination axis: how far can this organism travel through air.

Contact does not travel through air at all. Droplet travels a few feet and falls. Airborne stays up and drifts.

That is the entire logic. Everything else is a consequence of it.

Contact: it moves on hands

Names: MRSA, VRE, C. difficile, scabies, lice, norovirus, RSV, impetigo, draining wounds, any multidrug-resistant organism.

Wear: gown and gloves, put on before you enter the room.

Room: private, or cohort with patients who have the same organism. Stethoscope and cuff stay in the room.

Droplet: it falls out of the air

Names: influenza, pertussis, mumps, rubella, meningococcal disease, group A streptococcus, diphtheria, Haemophilus influenzae type b.

Wear: surgical mask on entry. Add eye protection if splashing is likely.

Room: private preferred, and normal ventilation is fine. The door can stay open.

Airborne: it stays suspended

Names: tuberculosis, measles, varicella, disseminated herpes zoster.

Wear: fit-tested N95 respirator or higher, put on before entry and removed after you leave.

Room: negative-pressure airborne infection isolation room, door closed at all times.

Protective: keep the world out

Who: severely immunocompromised patients, classically after a stem cell transplant.

Wear: what you bring in matters more than what you take out. No visitors with any illness.

Room: positive pressure with filtered air. No standing water, no fresh flowers, no live plants.

The axis that separates them

Distance, and whether the particle can hang in the air.

A droplet is heavy. It travels roughly 3 to 6 feet, then gravity wins, which is why a surgical mask on entry is enough and why the door can be open.

An airborne particle is light enough to stay up and move with the air, so a surgical mask is not enough and the room has to hold the air in.

The ones that carry two labels

ConditionPrecautionsWhy both
Varicella, chickenpoxAirborne and contactIt is in the air and in the fluid inside the lesions
Disseminated zoster, or zoster in an immunocompromised patientAirborne and contactWidespread lesions behave like chickenpox
Localised zoster in a healthy patientStandard, with the lesions coveredIntact covering contains it, so the route closes
RSV and most childhood respiratory virusesContact and dropletHands and secretions do the work together
AdenovirusContact and dropletSame reason, and it survives on surfaces
Draining, uncontained abscessContactThe drainage is the reservoir. Contained and dry drops it back to standard
Which way the air moves

Two rooms, opposite jobs, and one of the most reliable exam distinctions in this topic.

  • Negative pressure. Air flows into the room and is exhausted outside or filtered. It protects the unit from the patient. Tuberculosis, measles, varicella
  • Positive pressure. Filtered air flows out of the room. It protects the patient from the unit. Stem cell transplant, profound neutropenia

Ask who is being protected and the room chooses itself. The door stays closed either way.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Sort the Route Before You Read the Options
Cover the answers. Say contact, droplet or airborne out loud from the stem. Then read. Most of the wrong options are the right equipment for the wrong route, and they stop looking tempting once you have already committed to the route.

Transport, and the mask that moves

Leaving the room

Patients on precautions still need scans and procedures. Limit the trips, and prepare them.

  • Droplet and airborne: the patient wears a surgical mask, not an N95
  • Contact: cover the wound or the affected skin with clean dressings
  • Call ahead so the receiving department is ready
  • You wear clean equipment, and you take off the contaminated gown and gloves before pushing the chair

The mask moves from you to the patient. That flip is worth remembering, because it is asked often.

Bottom Line: Contact is touch, droplet is a few feet, airborne floats. Route decides room, room decides equipment, and the patient wears the surgical mask on the way out.
06

Equipment, in the right order

Both orders are testable, and one is far more testable than the other.

Going in, you build from the inside out. Coming out, you remove the dirtiest thing first.

Putting it on
  • Hand hygiene
  • Gown, tied at neck and waist
  • Mask or respirator, with a seal check for an N95
  • Goggles or face shield
  • Gloves, pulled over the gown cuffs

Logic: gloves last, so they cover the cuff and nothing you touch afterwards is bare skin.

Taking it off
  • Gloves, peeled off without touching the outside
  • Goggles or face shield, handled by the strap
  • Gown, rolled away from you and discarded
  • Leave the room, then remove the mask or respirator by the ties
  • Hand hygiene

Logic: gloves are the dirtiest. The respirator comes off outside because the air inside is what you were protecting yourself from.

The two rules that make the order obvious

You do not have to memorise two lists if you hold two ideas.

  • Gloves are always dirtiest. They go on last and come off first
  • Never touch the front. The outside of everything is contaminated. Straps, ties and the inside are the clean parts you handle

Hand hygiene goes at both ends, and again any time your hands become contaminated mid-sequence.

The N95, specifically

A respirator only works if it seals
  • Fit testing is done before first use and repeated at least annually
  • A seal check happens every single time you put one on: breathe in, the mask should pull inward
  • Facial hair along the seal breaks it, so the seal is the reason for the rule, not appearance
  • A surgical mask filters nothing at the edges. It is not an alternative for airborne organisms

A powered air-purifying respirator is used when a seal is not achievable.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Gloves Off First, Respirator Off Outside
Two anchors get you the whole doffing sequence. Gloves come off first because they are dirtiest. The respirator comes off after you have left the room and shut the door, because the reason you wore it is still in there. Everything in between removes by strap or tie, never by the front.
Bottom Line: Gown, mask, eyes, gloves going in. Gloves, eyes, gown, then out the door and mask off. Hand hygiene bookends both.
07

Clean or sterile

Discrimination axis: are you reducing organisms, or removing all of them.

Medical asepsis reduces the number of organisms. Surgical asepsis removes them entirely from a field.

Questions test which one a task needs, and then whether you broke it.

TypeGoalWhere you use it
Medical asepsis, clean techniqueReduce and contain organismsHand hygiene, gloves for routine care, cleaning surfaces, oral medications, most dressing changes on the floor
Surgical asepsis, sterile techniqueKeep an area free of all organismsUrinary catheter insertion, central line dressing and insertion, injections and intravenous fluid, surgery, tracheostomy care, any entry into a sterile body cavity
Sterile field rules that get tested
  • The outer 1 inch border of the field is contaminated. Nothing sterile is placed there
  • Anything below waist level is contaminated, even if it was sterile a second ago
  • Never turn your back on the field, and never reach across it
  • Moisture wicks organisms upward, so a wet field is a contaminated field
  • Hold sterile items above waist level and in front of you
  • Open the first flap of a package away from you, then the sides, then toward you
  • If you are unsure whether something is sterile, it is not
The axis that separates them

Where the item is going. Skin and mucous membranes that already have organisms take clean technique.

Anywhere sterile in the body, or any device that will sit inside it, takes sterile technique. A catheter enters a sterile bladder, so the insertion is sterile even though the urethra is not.

Bottom Line: Clean reduces, sterile eliminates. Border, waist and moisture are the three ways students break a field.
08

The four device infections

Most hospital-acquired infections come from four sources, and each one has a device or a procedure behind it.

For each, know the one prevention that matters most.

CAUTI
Catheter-associated urinary tract infection

The catheter is a highway into a sterile bladder, and risk rises with every day it stays.

Prevention: do not insert it without a real indication, and remove it as early as possible. Sterile insertion, closed system, bag below the bladder and off the floor, no dependent loops.

CLABSI
Central line-associated bloodstream infection

A line straight into the central circulation, past every skin defence.

Prevention: full barrier precautions at insertion, chlorhexidine skin prep, scrub the hub before every access, dressing changed on schedule and whenever it is soiled or loose, daily review of whether the line is still needed.

VAP
Ventilator-associated pneumonia

The tube holds the airway open in both directions.

Prevention: head of bed at 30 to 45 degrees, oral care with chlorhexidine, daily sedation interruption and readiness-to-wean assessment, subglottic suctioning.

SSI
Surgical site infection

The wound is a portal of entry for as long as it is open.

Prevention: antibiotic within the hour before incision, clippers rather than a razor, normothermia and glucose control during surgery, sterile dressing kept intact for the first 24 to 48 hours.

The common thread

Every one of these gets better when the device comes out.

So the highest-yield nursing action across all four is the same: ask every day whether this line, tube or catheter is still needed, and advocate for removal when it is not.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
The Best Line Is the One That Came Out
When a question asks how to reduce catheter or line infections and one option is early removal or daily review of need, take it. Meticulous care of a device that should not be there is the option that sounds diligent and scores lower.

Antibiotic-resistant organisms

What changes at the bedside
  • MRSA and VRE. Contact precautions, dedicated equipment, private room or cohorting
  • C. difficile. Contact precautions, soap and water, bleach-based cleaning. Watch for the abdomen becoming distended and tender, which can mean toxic megacolon
  • Multidrug-resistant gram-negatives. Contact precautions and careful antibiotic stewardship

Finishing the full antibiotic course, and not pressing for antibiotics for viral illness, is patient teaching that gets tested.

Bottom Line: Four devices, four bundles, one shared answer. Take the device out as soon as the reason for it is gone.
09

Protecting the patient instead

Discrimination axis: who is being protected, the unit or the patient.

Everything so far keeps organisms in. This section is the mirror image.

A neutropenic patient cannot mount a normal response, so the usual signs are missing.

Fever may be the only sign

No pus, no redness, no swelling. Those signs are made of neutrophils, and this patient does not have them.

So a single temperature of 38.0 degrees Celsius or above in a neutropenic patient is an emergency, not an observation. Blood cultures and broad-spectrum antibiotics go in fast.

An absolute neutrophil count under 500 is severe neutropenia. That number is what triggers the whole protective package.

What protective precautions actually involve
  • Private room, positive pressure with filtered air where available
  • Meticulous hand hygiene by everyone entering, which is the main protection
  • No visitors who are ill, and no recent live-vaccine recipients
  • No fresh flowers, no live plants, no standing water in the room
  • No raw or undercooked food, and thorough washing of fruit and vegetables
  • Daily bathing, scrupulous oral care with a soft brush, and perineal care
  • Avoid rectal temperatures, suppositories and enemas, because the mucosa tears
Children on chemotherapy

Live vaccines wait. A child receiving chemotherapy does not get varicella or MMR, and household contacts need careful planning.

  • Chickenpox exposure is urgent, so the family calls the same day rather than waiting
  • School return is decided on counts, not on how well the child seems
  • Siblings can receive inactivated influenza vaccine, which protects the patient too
  • Fever teaching goes to the parent in numbers, with a thermometer and a phone number
FlorenceBot Pro Tip
A Quiet Wound Is Not a Clean Wound
In a neutropenic patient, absent redness and absent pus mean nothing. They cannot make either. So a temperature that would be routine anywhere else is the emergency here, and the answer is culture and treat, not observe and recheck.
Bottom Line: Same room design, opposite airflow, opposite goal. Fever alone is the whole assessment in a neutropenic patient.
10

What you do first

Priority questions hand you four reasonable actions. All four are things a nurse would do.

You are being tested on order, not on correctness.

The ordering rules
  • Hand hygiene comes before and after everything. If it is an option and the question is about transmission, it is usually the answer
  • Equipment goes on before you enter, not once you are at the bedside
  • When the organism is unknown but the picture is suspicious, use the higher level of precaution and step down later
  • Protect the room first, then the patient's dignity. Both matter, and one is time critical
  • Assess before you escalate, unless the finding is already an emergency
  • Report and document exposures immediately. A needlestick has a clock on it

Suspected tuberculosis, in order

  1. 1
    Put the patient in a negative-pressure room with the door closed. Do this before the diagnosis is confirmed, not after.
  2. 2
    Wear a fit-tested N95 for every entry, and seal check it every time.
  3. 3
    Collect sputum as ordered and teach the patient to cover coughs. Sputum collection is a high-risk moment for everyone in the room.
  4. 4
    Limit transport. When it has to happen, the patient wears a surgical mask and the receiving unit is warned.
  5. 5
    Teach adherence. Treatment is months long, and stopping early is how resistance is made. Directly observed therapy exists for this reason.
After a needlestick

The order here is fixed, and it is asked.

  • Wash the site with soap and water immediately. Flush mucous membranes with water or saline
  • Report it to your supervisor and to occupational health right away
  • Source and employee testing as directed
  • Post-exposure prophylaxis for HIV works best when started within hours, so this is not a next-shift problem

Never recap a needle. Activate the safety device and drop it in the sharps container yourself, at the point of use.

Isolation has a cost

Patients in isolation get seen less often, and they know it. Depression, anxiety and loneliness are documented consequences.

So the plan includes it. Explain the reason in plain words, go in for reasons other than tasks, and set up contact with family.

An answer that keeps the precautions and addresses the isolation beats one that relaxes the precautions to be kind.

Bottom Line: Hand hygiene, then equipment before entry, then the higher precaution when in doubt. Exposures get reported the same hour.
