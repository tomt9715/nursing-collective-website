IV Medications

The drugs that punish speed, the complication you must escalate in seconds, and the fluid choice that is decided before you ever hang the bag.

By the end, without notes

  • Name the five high-alert IV drug groups and what an independent double check is
  • State the potassium rule in one sentence, and why it is a rule
  • Separate infiltration from extravasation, and say which one you escalate

01

What actually gets you

IV therapy is not conceptually hard. It punishes you on the details. The drug is right and the rate is wrong, and the patient is harmed anyway.

Three failure modes account for most of the lost points. Read them before anything else.

You treated potassium like any other additive

It is not. Concentrated potassium is never given by IV push. Ever. It must be diluted and run on a pump.

Pushed potassium stops the heart. It is one of the fastest killers on the medication cart. Units removed concentrated vials from floor stock for exactly this reason.

You could not tell infiltration from extravasation

Both look like a swollen arm. Only one causes tissue necrosis.

Infiltration is a harmless solution in the tissue. Extravasation is a vesicant in the tissue. If you cannot name which, you will not escalate the one that needs escalating in minutes.

You ran two drugs into one line without checking

Compatibility gets checked before you connect, not after. A precipitate in the tubing can embolize.

The question is rarely what the drug does. It is whether these two can share a line, and what you flush with in between.

Bottom Line: Study IV therapy as four discriminations: which drugs need a second nurse, infiltration or extravasation, which tonicity this patient needs, and peripheral or central.
02

The four things

For each topic area below you should be able to say four things with the book closed. What it is. The clue you would see. The priority action. The teaching or safety point.

If you cannot say all four out loud, you do not know it yet. Familiarity is not recall.

Topic area What it is in one sentence Clue in the scenario Priority action Teaching or safety point
High-alert drugs Drugs that cause severe harm when the dose or rate is wrong. Insulin, heparin, opioids, concentrated electrolytes, chemotherapy. Independent double check before it goes in. Two nurses check separately. Concentrated potassium is never pushed.
Complications The infusion leaves the vein, inflames it, or overwhelms the patient. Swelling, a red cord, sudden dyspnea, crackles. Stop the infusion first, then assess. Extravasation is the emergency. Escalate it, do not watch it.
Fluid choice Tonicity decides which way water moves across the cell membrane. Hypovolemia, cellular dehydration, or cerebral edema. Match the fluid to the compartment that is empty. Never hang hypotonic fluid in a head injury.

03

High-alert medications

Discrimination axis: does an error here cause severe harm, or only an incorrect dose.

High-alert does not mean the drug is used often or is hard to give. It means the consequence of an error is severe. That is the whole definition.

GroupExamplesWhat goes wrongYour safeguard
InsulinRegular insulin infusion, insulin bolusesLook-alike names and units misread as volumeIndependent double check. Regular is the standard insulin for infusion.
AnticoagulantsHeparin, argatrobanUnits versus milligrams; wrong pump rateDouble check the concentration and the rate. Monitor for bleeding.
OpioidsMorphine, hydromorphone, fentanylGiven too fast, or the wrong opioid at the same numberPush slowly. Hydromorphone is far more potent than morphine per milligram.
Concentrated electrolytesPotassium chloride, hypertonic saline, magnesiumUndiluted or infused too fastDilute, use a pump, and never push concentrated potassium.
ChemotherapyVincristine, doxorubicinWrong route, or a vesicant into tissueVerify the route out loud. Vincristine is IV only.

What an independent double check actually is

Two nurses check the same thing separately. Separately is the operative word.

One nurse does not read the numbers out while the other nods. Each verifies the order, the drug, the concentration, the dose, the pump programming and the line. Then they compare. If the second nurse hears the answer first, it is not independent, and the check is worthless.

The potassium rule

Concentrated potassium chloride is never given by IV push. It is always diluted and always run on a pump.

The usual ceiling is 10 mEq per hour. Faster than that needs continuous cardiac monitoring, and even then it is capped around 20 mEq per hour by policy.

Potassium burns going in. Peripheral infusion causes real pain, and more concentrated solutions belong in a central line. Never add potassium to a bag that is already hanging.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Potassium Is Never Pushed
Any answer option that pushes potassium is wrong before you read the rest of it. Dilute, pump, 10 mEq per hour, and never add it to a hanging bag. If a scenario hands you a potassium syringe, the correct action is to question the order.

Insulin, specifically

Regular insulin is the standard insulin for an IV infusion. Some rapid-acting analogs are labeled for IV use under close monitoring, but hospital protocols use regular. NPH, premixes and long-acting insulins never go IV.

Insulin is high-alert, so the infusion gets an independent double check. In diabetic ketoacidosis the order is fixed. Fluids first. Then potassium. Then the insulin infusion.

Hold the insulin if the potassium is under 3.3. Insulin drives potassium into the cells, and a level that is already low will fall further. Correct the potassium first, then start the drip.

Route errors are the fatal ones

Vincristine is IV only. Given intrathecally it is fatal, and there is no reversal for that error. Pharmacy dispenses it in a minibag rather than a syringe for exactly this reason.

Extravasation is a different problem with a different answer. For a vinca alkaloid into tissue there is an antidote: hyaluronidase, with a warm compress.

Read the label. Say the route out loud. Refuse a syringe that does not name its route.

04

When the line goes wrong

This is the highest-yield section in the guide. Every complication below has a look-alike. Learn the axis, not the list.

Infiltration or extravasation

Discrimination axis: is the leaked solution a vesicant.

Both mean the fluid is in the tissue instead of the vein. The findings can look identical. What separates them is the drug, and the drug decides the urgency.

Infiltration: a non-vesicant in the tissue
  • Skin is cool, pale and taut
  • Swelling around the site
  • Infusion slows or the pump alarms
  • No blood return
  • Dull discomfort, not burning

What you do: stop the infusion. Remove the catheter. Elevate the limb. Apply a warm or cool compress based on the solution.

It usually resolves. It is not an emergency.

Extravasation: a vesicant in the tissue
  • Burning or stinging at the site
  • Swelling, then blistering
  • Skin may darken or slough over days
  • Same cool taut swelling as infiltration
  • The difference is the drug, not the finding

What you do: stop immediately. Disconnect the tubing, leave the catheter, and aspirate residual drug from it. Do not flush.

Then notify the provider now. Mark the border and document.

The axis that separates them

Vesicant or not. That is the entire distinction.

Vesicants blister and kill tissue. The common ones are chemotherapy such as vincristine and doxorubicin, vasopressors such as norepinephrine and dopamine, concentrated potassium, calcium, phenytoin, promethazine and hypertonic saline.

An infiltration is managed. An extravasation is escalated. Antidotes exist and they are time sensitive, so the provider needs to know within minutes.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Vesicant Makes It an Emergency
Do not sort these by how the arm looks. Sort them by what was running. Saline in the tissue is an infiltration. Norepinephrine in the tissue is an extravasation, and it needs a provider now. Aspirate before you pull the catheter, and never flush a suspected extravasation.

Phlebitis or infiltration

Discrimination axis: red and warm along the vein, or cool and swollen around it.

These two get mixed up constantly. The findings are opposites once you look for the right thing.

Phlebitis: the vein is inflamed
  • Redness that tracks along the vein
  • Warmth and tenderness
  • A palpable cord you can feel
  • Swelling is minimal

What you do: discontinue and restart in a different site. Warm compress. Document the grade.

Causes are the catheter itself, the drug pH, or an infusion running too fast.

Infiltration: the fluid is outside the vein
  • Cool, pale, tight skin
  • Swelling around the site
  • No red streak
  • No blood return

What you do: stop, remove, elevate, compress.

Compare limbs if you are unsure. The difference in circumference is usually obvious.

Two words that settle it

Red cord means phlebitis. Cool swelling means infiltration.

Warm and red is the vein reacting. Cool and puffy is the fluid escaping. Both come out; only one leaves a streak.

The systemic complications

These do not stay at the site. They present as a patient who suddenly changes.

ComplicationWhat you seeWhat you do firstWhy
Local infection Redness, warmth, purulent drainage at the site, fever Discontinue the line and culture as ordered The catheter is the source until proven otherwise
Bloodstream infection Fever, chills, hypotension with a central line in place Cultures, notify, and expect line removal Central catheters seed the bloodstream directly
Air embolism Sudden dyspnea, chest pain, hypotension, confusion Clamp the line, then position left side with the head down Trapping air in the right ventricle keeps it out of the lungs
Fluid overload Crackles, dyspnea, distended neck veins, bounding pulse Slow the infusion to keep the vein open, sit them up Volume arrived faster than the heart can move it
Speed shock Flushed face, pounding headache, tight chest, irregular pulse Stop the drug, keep the line open with saline A bolus of drug hit the circulation all at once
Air embolism position

Left side, head down. Clamp the line first, then turn them.

The left lateral Trendelenburg position traps air at the apex of the right ventricle, away from the pulmonary outflow. Give oxygen and notify the provider. Then find how the air got in.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Left Side, Head Down
Sudden dyspnea in a patient with a central line is an air embolism until proven otherwise. Clamp, turn them left side with the head down, oxygen, call. Prevention is easier: keep the line clamped during tubing changes and put an occlusive dressing on after removal.
Write this down

Blank paper. Three columns: infiltration, extravasation, phlebitis. Write the skin finding, the temperature of the skin, and the first action. Then add the one word that separates each pair.

05

Which fluid, and why

Discrimination axis: which way does water move across the cell membrane.

Tonicity is not trivia. It tells you where the water goes. Pick the compartment that is empty, then pick the fluid that fills it.

Isotonic: stays where you put it

0.9 percent sodium chloride, lactated Ringers. Same tonicity as plasma, so the cell neither swells nor shrinks.

These are the workhorses. Use them for hypovolemia, hemorrhage, dehydration and shock.

Normal saline is the only fluid compatible with blood. Lactated Ringers is the resuscitation fluid in burns.

Watch for: fluid overload. It all stays in the vascular space.

Hypotonic: pushes water into the cell

0.45 percent sodium chloride. Lower tonicity than plasma, so water moves into the cells and swells them.

Use it when the cells are dry: hypernatremia, cellular dehydration, and later maintenance in ketoacidosis.

Never in a head injury. Swelling brain cells is the last thing that patient needs.

Watch for: falling blood pressure. You are pulling volume out of the vessels.

Hypertonic: pulls water out of the cell

3 percent sodium chloride, dextrose 5 percent in 0.9 percent saline. Higher tonicity than plasma, so water leaves the cells.

Use it for severe symptomatic hyponatremia and cerebral edema.

Give small volumes on a pump. Recheck sodium often. Correcting sodium too fast causes its own brain injury.

Watch for: fluid overload and a rapidly rising sodium.

The axis that separates them

Ask one question. Where is the fluid missing.

Missing from the vessels means isotonic. Missing from inside the cells means hypotonic. Too much water inside the cells means hypertonic.

Dextrose 5 percent in water is the exception worth remembering. It is isotonic in the bag and behaves as free water once the dextrose is metabolized.

Choosing between the two workhorses

FluidReach for it whenBe careful when
0.9 percent sodium chlorideBlood transfusion, hyponatremia, most drug dilutions, flushesLarge volumes can cause a hyperchloremic acidosis
Lactated RingersBurns, surgery, large-volume resuscitation, gastrointestinal lossesIt contains potassium, so use caution in renal failure and hyperkalemia. The liver must convert the lactate.

Burn resuscitation uses lactated Ringers. The Parkland formula is 4 mL times kilograms times percent burned. Half of that goes in the first 8 hours, timed from the burn, then you titrate to urine output.

In sickle cell crisis the fluids run at 1.5 to 2 times maintenance. In tumor lysis prevention they run at 2 to 3 times maintenance. The fluid is the treatment in both.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Hypotonic and the Brain
Hypotonic fluid swells cells, and a brain has nowhere to swell. Head injury, stroke and raised intracranial pressure all rule it out. If a scenario pairs a head injury with a hypotonic bag, that is the wrong answer they planted.
06

Peripheral or central

Discrimination axis: will this solution damage a small vein.

Access is not a comfort decision. It is decided by what you plan to infuse and for how long.

Four things send you central
  • Vesicants. Chemotherapy and vasopressors need a large-flow vein.
  • Parenteral nutrition. The osmolarity destroys peripheral veins.
  • Long duration. Weeks of therapy, or repeated blood draws.
  • Extreme pH or osmolarity. A pH under 5 or over 9, or osmolarity above roughly 900 mOsm per litre.

Any one of the four is enough. Blood in a big vein dilutes the irritant almost instantly.

Peripheral: short catheter, arm vein

Best for short courses of non-irritating solutions.

Use the forearm. Avoid areas of flexion, the wrist, and the leg in adults.

Avoid the arm on the side of a mastectomy, a dialysis fistula, or paralysis after a stroke.

Gauge: 18 for blood and trauma, 20 for most adults, 22 or 24 for fragile veins and children.

Central: tip in the superior vena cava

PICC lines, tunneled catheters, implanted ports and non-tunneled central lines.

The tip sits near the junction of the vena cava and the right atrium. Placement is confirmed by x-ray before first use unless tip-locating technology was used.

A midline sits in between. It is longer than a peripheral, but the tip is not central, so it is not for vesicants or parenteral nutrition.

Caring for the line you have

TaskHowWhy it matters

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Vesicants Go Central
If the question hangs a vasopressor or parenteral nutrition in a hand vein, that is the error. Vesicant, nutrition, weeks of therapy, extreme pH. Any one sends you central, and a midline does not count as central.
07

Giving it safely

IV drugs skip absorption entirely. There is no gut to slow them and nothing to pump back out. Speed is the variable you control.

Why fast is dangerous

The recommended push rate exists because of a specific harm, not caution in general. Learn the harm and the rate sticks.

DrugRateWhat happens if you rush it
FurosemideNo faster than 20 mg per minuteHearing loss and ringing; the damage can be permanent
PhenytoinNo faster than 50 mg per minute, saline onlyHypotension and arrhythmia; it precipitates in dextrose
Vancomycin10 mg/min or at least 60 minutes, whichever is longer. 2 grams needs at least 120 minutesFlushing of the face and neck with hypotension
MorphineSlow push over several minutesRespiratory depression and hypotension
Adenosine1 to 2 second push, then a 20 mL saline flushIt fails; the half-life is seconds, so slow is useless
Concentrated potassiumNever pushed; 10 mEq per hour on a pumpCardiac arrest

Adenosine is the exception that proves the rule. Almost everything else is harmed by speed. Adenosine is destroyed by slowness.

The rights, and the two that get skipped

  1. 1
    Right patient. Two identifiers, from the band and from the patient. The room number is not an identifier.
  2. 2
    Right drug. Read the full generic name. Look-alike names are how insulin errors start.
  3. 3
    Right dose. Check the concentration, not just the volume. Units are not millilitres.
  4. 4
    Right route. Say it out loud. This is the check that prevents the fatal chemotherapy error.
  5. 5
    Right time. Including the rate and the duration, which are part of the order.
  6. 6
    Right reason. Know why this patient is getting this drug. This one gets skipped.
  7. 7
    Right response. Reassess after you give it. This one gets skipped too, and it is where the documentation lives.

Compatibility, labelling and the equipment

Before you connect anything
  • Check Y-site compatibility for every pair sharing a line. Check it before you connect.
  • Flush with saline between drugs when compatibility is uncertain.
  • Cloudiness, colour change, gas or crystals mean stop. Do not infuse it.
  • Phenytoin goes in saline only. Blood goes with saline only.
  • Label the bag and the tubing with the drug, the date and your initials.
EquipmentWhat to know
Smart pumpsUse the drug library so the dose limits apply. Overriding a soft limit is a decision you must be able to defend.
Free-flow protectionUse administration sets that clamp automatically when removed from the pump. Close the roller clamp before you open the door.
FiltersBlood runs through an in-line filter. Parenteral nutrition uses a filter, larger pore size when lipids are included. Use a filter needle to draw from a glass ampule.
Tubing changesContinuous sets no more often than every 96 hours. Intermittent sets every 24 hours. Blood tubing after 4 hours. Standalone fat emulsion sets every 12 hours, and 3-in-1 parenteral nutrition every 24 hours.
FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Scrub the Hub, Then Wait
Fifteen seconds of friction, then let it dry. Most people do the scrub and skip the drying, and the antiseptic has not finished working. The dry time is the part that kills organisms, not the wipe.
08

Blood, the short version

Blood is an IV product with its own rules. The rules are short and they are absolute.

  1. 1
    Before. Confirm consent, type and crossmatch, and a patent line of 20 gauge or larger. Take baseline vital signs. Blood tubing has an in-line filter and gets primed with normal saline.
  2. 2
    Verify with a second person. Two qualified staff check the patient identifiers, the blood type and Rh, the unit number and the expiration, at the bedside. Not at the desk.
  3. 3
    Start slowly and stay. Remain with the patient for the first 15 minutes. Most severe reactions declare themselves in that window.
  4. 4
    Then monitor. Recheck vital signs at 15 minutes and per policy after that. Finish each unit within 4 hours.
  5. 5
    If anything changes. Stop the blood. Keep the line open with normal saline through new tubing. Notify the provider and the blood bank. Send the bag, the tubing and the samples back.
Saline only

Normal saline is the only solution that goes with blood.

Dextrose lyses red cells. Lactated Ringers contains calcium, which can clot the unit. No drug is ever added to a blood product.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
The First 15 Minutes
Start slow and do not leave the room. Fever, chills, back pain, flank pain or a drop in pressure means stop. Stop first, then keep the line open with saline through new tubing, then call. Stopping is always the first action, never the assessment.
09

Rate math you cannot fumble

Two formulas cover nearly everything. Learn them as sentences, not symbols.

You needFormulaRound to
mL per hour, for a pumpTotal volume in mL divided by the number of hoursThe nearest whole millilitre
Drops per minute, by gravityVolume in mL times the drop factor, divided by the time in minutesThe nearest whole drop

Worked example one

The order is 1000 mL of normal saline over 8 hours. The tubing is 15 gtt per mL.

For the pump: 1000 divided by 8 is 125 mL per hour.

For gravity: 8 hours is 480 minutes. 1000 times 15 is 15,000. Divide by 480 and you get 31.25. You set 31 drops per minute.

Worked example two

The order is an antibiotic in 50 mL over 30 minutes. The tubing is 10 gtt per mL.

For the pump: 50 mL in half an hour is 100 mL per hour.

For gravity: 50 times 10 is 500. Divide by 30 and you get 16.7. You set 17 drops per minute.

When the order is weight based

Some infusions are ordered in mcg per kilogram per minute. Convert to mL per hour in three steps.

Take dopamine 400 mg in 250 mL. That is 1600 mcg per mL. An 80 kg patient at 5 mcg per kg per minute needs 400 mcg per minute, which is 24,000 mcg per hour. Divide by 1600 and the pump gets 15 mL per hour.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Minutes on the Bottom
Drops per minute needs minutes in the denominator, so convert hours first. The single most common error is dividing by 8 instead of 480. Sanity check the answer: a drip you can count should land somewhere between about 10 and 60 per minute.
Copy this down

Write both formulas by hand, then work the two examples again on a fresh sheet. Do not read the worked answer first. Copying a solved problem teaches nothing.

10

What you do first

Priority questions hand you four reasonable actions. All four are things a nurse does. You are being tested on ordering.

The ordering rules
  • Stop the infusion first, then assess. You cannot assess your way out of a drug still going in.
  • Check compatibility before you connect, not after you see cloudiness.
  • Assess the site before every dose, not once a shift and hope.
  • Escalate an extravasation immediately. Antidotes are time dependent.
  • Question the order before you give it. Once it is in, you cannot take it back.

Stop first, in practice

The same sequence covers most IV emergencies. Learn it once.

  1. 1
    Stop the infusion. Clamp it or turn off the pump. This is always first, and it needs no order.
  2. 2
    Protect the access. For a reaction, keep the vein open with normal saline through new tubing. For a suspected extravasation, leave the catheter and aspirate rather than flush.
  3. 3
    Assess the patient. Airway, breathing, circulation, then the site. A sudden change is systemic until you rule it out.
  4. 4
    Notify. The provider, and the blood bank or pharmacy where relevant. Say what you stopped and when.
  5. 5
    Document and preserve. Keep the bag and the tubing. Mark the borders of a swollen area. Record the time, the volume in, and what you saw.
The one exception to stop first

Fluid overload does not get the line clamped shut. Slow it to keep the vein open instead.

You still need that access for a diuretic. Sit the patient up, give oxygen, and call.

Orders you should know cold

Ketoacidosis: fluids, then potassium, then the insulin infusion. Hold the insulin if the potassium is under 3.3.

Hyperkalemia with heart changes: calcium gluconate first to protect the heart, then insulin and glucose to shift it, then a binder or dialysis to remove it.

Both are the same idea. Protect the patient before you correct the number.
