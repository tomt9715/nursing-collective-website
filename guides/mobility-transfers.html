Mobility and Transfers

Which side you stand on, which leg goes first, and why the patient who has not moved in three days is the sickest one on your list.

By the end, without notes

  • Say which side of the patient you stand on, and why
  • Place a cane and a walker correctly, and measure crutches
  • Give the stairs rule for a patient with one weak leg
  • Name the position for six common situations, without hesitating
  • List the complications of immobility by body system, and the prevention for each

01

What actually gets you

This topic looks like common sense. It is full of specific rules that do not feel like rules.

Points go in four places.

You guessed which side to stand on

You stand on the weaker side. Always.

That is the side they will fall toward, and it is the side that cannot catch them. The cane goes in the other hand, so the strong arm and the weak leg work together.

You stood them up straight from lying down

Bed rest blunts the reflex that holds your blood pressure up when you stand.

So the sequence is lie, sit, dangle, stand. Skipping the dangle is how a patient goes down in the first three seconds of a transfer.

You lifted with your back and your goodwill

Good body mechanics do not make a heavy patient safe to lift by hand.

Above about 35 pounds of patient weight, equipment and extra people are the answer. Nurse injuries end careers, and the patient goes down with you.

You treated immobility as a comfort problem

It is a whole-body illness with a clock on it.

Clots, pneumonia, pressure injuries, constipation, contractures and bone loss all start within days. The intervention is almost always to get them moving sooner.

Bottom Line: Weaker side, dangle first, equipment over willpower, and move them earlier than feels necessary.
02

The four things

Four tasks carry this topic. For each one you should be able to say four things with the book closed.

What it is. What you set up first. The rule that decides the answer. And the error people make.

Task What it is Set up first The deciding rule The error
Transfer Moving a patient between bed, chair, commode or stretcher. Bed low, wheels locked, chair on the strong side, gait belt on, shoes on. Dangle before standing. Stand on the weaker side. Pulling under the arms, which dislocates shoulders.
Ambulating with a device Walking with a cane, walker or crutches. Fit and measure the device. Clear the path. Belt on. Cane opposite the weak leg. Weak leg moves with the device. Putting the cane on the injured side.
Positioning Placing the body to protect airway, skin, joints and circulation. Know why you are positioning before you choose one. The position follows the problem, not the routine. Full 90-degree side-lying, which crushes the trochanter.
Preventing immobility harm Everything that goes wrong when a body stops moving. A turning schedule, a skin risk score, and early ambulation. Reposition every 2 hours in bed, every hour in a chair. Treating early mobility as optional once the patient is comfortable.

03

Moving people without wrecking yourself

Discrimination axis: is this a lift, or is it a slide.

Lifting is what injures backs. Sliding, rolling and pivoting are what you do instead.

The mechanics that matter
  • Feet apart, one slightly forward, for a wide base of support
  • Bend at the hips and knees, never at the waist
  • Keep the load close to your body and centred
  • Push, pull or slide rather than lift
  • Pivot with your feet. Never twist your spine
  • Raise the bed to your waist height before you start
  • Count out loud so everyone moves on the same beat
  • Face the direction of the move
The limit that overrides technique

Manual lifting of more than about 35 pounds of patient weight is unsafe no matter how good your form is.

Past that you use a friction-reducing sheet, a slide board, a sit-to-stand device, a ceiling or floor lift, or more people.

Never drag a patient across a sheet. Friction and shear tear skin, and shear is what turns pressure into a deep injury.

The gait belt
  • Around the waist, over clothing, snug enough for a flat hand to fit underneath
  • Grip underhanded, from below the belt
  • Not used over a fresh abdominal incision, a feeding tube, an ostomy or fractured ribs
  • If the patient starts to go down, do not fight it. Guide them down your leg to the floor and protect the head

Never lift a patient by the arms or under the axillae. That is how shoulders dislocate.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Equipment Beats Technique
When a question describes a heavy or dependent patient, the correct answer usually involves a device or more staff, not better body mechanics. Good form does not raise the weight limit, and any option that has one nurse lifting alone is wrong.
Bottom Line: Wide base, bend the knees, keep it close, pivot the feet. Past 35 pounds, get equipment.
04

Bed to chair, in order

Discrimination axis: how much can this patient do themselves.

Assess that before you choose the method. It decides everything that follows.

  1. 1
    Assess. Strength, weight bearing status, balance, cognition, pain, dizziness, and whether they did this yesterday.
  2. 2
    Set up the room. Bed in the lowest position, wheels locked, chair on the patient's strong side, footrests out of the way, path clear.
  3. 3
    Dress the feet. Non-skid footwear before anything else. Bare feet and loose socks are a fall.
  4. 4
    Sit them up and dangle. Feet flat on the floor, sitting on the edge, for one to five minutes. Ask about dizziness and watch their colour.
  5. 5
    Apply the gait belt and position yourself on the weaker side, facing the patient, with your knees against theirs.
  6. 6
    Stand on a count. They push from the mattress rather than pulling on you. Then pivot with small steps, never twisting.
  7. 7
    Lower them with control until they feel the chair behind their knees. Then call light, alignment, and reassess.
The strong side leads

Two rules that look contradictory until you see the logic.

  • You stand on the weak side. That is where they will fall, and where they have no support
  • The chair goes on the strong side. They move toward it with the leg that works, and pivot on the strong foot

Same principle both times. The weak side gets your help. The strong side does the work.

Orthostatic hypotension

A drop of 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic on standing, often with a rise in heart rate.

If the patient goes pale, says the room is moving, or their pressure drops, sit them straight back down. Do not walk them through it.

Risk is highest after bed rest, after opioids or antihypertensives, and in anyone dehydrated or bleeding.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Lie, Sit, Dangle, Stand
Four steps, and the third is the one that gets skipped in the answer options. If a stem has a patient who has been in bed for days, the option that includes sitting on the edge first is almost always correct.
Bottom Line: Assess, set up, shoes, dangle, belt, stand, pivot. You on the weak side, chair on the strong side.
05

Canes, walkers and crutches

Discrimination axis: how much weight has to come off the leg.

A cane offloads a little. A walker offloads more. Crutches can take everything.

Cane
  • Held on the strong side, opposite the weak leg
  • Tip about 6 inches to the side of the strong foot
  • Handle level with the wrist crease when the arm hangs down
  • Elbow bends about 15 to 30 degrees
  • Cane and weak leg move together, then the strong leg

Memory: cane opposite the affected leg.

Walker
  • Handgrips level with the wrist crease, elbows about 15 to 30 degrees
  • Move the walker forward a short distance, then the weak leg, then the strong leg
  • All four points down before any weight goes through it
  • Stand inside the frame, not behind it
  • Never used to pull yourself up from a chair, and never on stairs

The error: walking too far behind it, which pulls the patient forward.

Crutches
  • Pads sit 2 to 3 finger widths below the axilla
  • Elbows bend about 20 to 30 degrees at the handgrips
  • Weight goes through the hands, never the armpits
  • Tripod position: tips about 6 inches ahead and 6 inches to the side
  • Rubber tips checked, and no loose rugs anywhere in the home

The danger: leaning on the pads compresses nerves and causes weakness in the hands.

Stairs, with any device
  • Up: strong leg first, then the weak leg and the device
  • Down: device and weak leg first, then the strong leg
  • Use the handrail on one side and the device on the other
  • A walker does not go on stairs

Memory: up with the good, down with the bad.

Crutch gaits, briefly
  • Four-point. Slow and stable, weight bearing on both legs
  • Three-point. One leg cannot bear weight, so both crutches and the weak leg move together
  • Two-point. Partial weight bearing on both legs, faster than four-point
  • Swing-through. Both legs move together past the crutches, used when both legs are weak

The gait follows the weight bearing order, so read the order before you pick the gait.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Opposite Hand, Same Step
The cane goes in the hand opposite the weak leg, and it moves at the same time as that leg. That single sentence answers most cane questions. For stairs, up with the good and down with the bad, every time, with every device.
Bottom Line: Cane opposite, walker in front, crutch weight through the hands. Up with the good, down with the bad.
06

Positions, and the problem each one solves

Do not memorise positions. Memorise the problem each one answers.

PositionWhat it looks likeWhen you use it
High FowlerHead of bed 60 to 90 degreesBreathlessness, eating, feeding through a tube, after some eye and head surgery
Semi-FowlerHead of bed 30 to 45 degreesPreventing aspiration and ventilator pneumonia, after most abdominal surgery, raised intracranial pressure
SupineFlat on the backSpinal alignment, after a spinal anesthetic in some protocols, resting position
ProneFace downImproving oxygenation in severe respiratory failure, and preventing hip flexion contracture
Lateral, 30 degreesSide-lying but tilted, supported with pillowsRoutine repositioning, because it keeps weight off the greater trochanter
SimsSemi-prone on the left side, upper knee flexedEnemas, rectal examination, and drainage from the mouth of an unconscious patient
TrendelenburgFlat with the feet raised above the headCentral line insertion and air embolism. It is not the routine answer for shock
Modified TrendelenburgFlat with the legs raisedHypotension, to return volume centrally without raising intracranial pressure
Why 30 degrees, twice

Two different 30-degree rules, and both are about shear.

  • Head of bed at or below 30 degrees when the situation allows, because higher than that the body slides down and the skin stays behind
  • Side-lying at 30 degrees rather than a full 90, because a true side-lying position puts all the weight on the greater trochanter

When aspiration risk requires a higher head of bed, aspiration wins. Then you protect the skin some other way.

Support and alignment
  • Pillows support, they do not prop. Under the calf, not under the knee, so the popliteal vessels stay open
  • Heels floated off the bed entirely. A heel is bone under skin with nothing in between
  • Feet supported to prevent foot drop, and hands positioned to prevent contracture
  • A trochanter roll along the outer thigh stops the leg rolling outward
  • No donut rings under the sacrum. They cut circulation in a circle around the wound
Bottom Line: Position follows the problem. Thirty degrees of head elevation and 30 degrees of tilt both exist to stop the skin shearing.
07

What immobility does, system by system

Every system suffers, and most of it starts within days.

Questions here give you a complication and ask for the prevention.

SystemWhat goes wrongWhat you do
CirculationVenous stasis, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, orthostatic dropsEarly ambulation, ankle pumps, compression devices, prophylactic anticoagulation as ordered
LungsSecretions pool, atelectasis, pneumoniaTurn, cough, deep breathe, incentive spirometer, sit them upright, get them walking
SkinPressure injury over bony points, shear damage, moisture damageReposition on a schedule, offload heels, keep skin dry, support surfaces
Muscle and jointStrength falls fast, contractures form, foot dropRange of motion at least twice daily, splints and foot support, sit them out of bed
BoneCalcium leaves the bone, so it weakens and calcium rises in the bloodWeight bearing where possible, hydration, movement
GutSlow transit, constipation, poor appetiteFluid, fibre, activity, a toileting routine, stool softeners if ordered
Kidney and bladderUrine pools when lying flat, so stasis, stones and infection followUpright to void, fluids, and get the catheter out early
MindSleep disruption, low mood, disorientation, loss of independenceDay and night structure, activity, involvement in care, family contact
The single best intervention

Getting the patient up. It answers seven of the eight rows above at once.

So when a question asks how to prevent a complication of immobility and one option is early ambulation or sitting out of bed, that option is usually correct.

Bed rest is a treatment with side effects. It needs a reason, and the reason needs reviewing daily.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Bed Rest Is a Drug With Side Effects
Treat it like a prescription that needs a reason and a review date. When the options offer early ambulation against a passive measure, early ambulation usually wins, because it addresses clots, lungs, gut, skin and mood at the same time.
Bottom Line: Eight systems, one answer that fixes most of them. Get them moving sooner than feels necessary.
08

Pressure injuries

Discrimination axis: can you see the base of the wound.

That question decides the stage, and staging decides the plan.

StageWhat you seeThe tell
Stage 1Intact skin with redness that does not blanch when pressedSkin is unbroken. In darker skin, look for a change in colour, temperature or firmness
Stage 2Partial thickness loss. A shallow open ulcer or an intact or ruptured blisterPink and moist. No slough, no fat visible
Stage 3Full thickness loss. Fat is visible, and it may tunnel or undermineYou can see fat, but not bone, tendon or muscle
Stage 4Full thickness loss exposing bone, tendon, muscle or cartilageStructure is visible or directly palpable
UnstageableBase covered by slough or escharYou cannot see the bottom, so you cannot stage it until it is debrided
Deep tissue injuryPersistent deep red, maroon or purple intact skin, or a blood-filled blisterDamage started underneath. It can open up quickly
The schedule
  • Reposition at least every 2 hours in bed
  • Reposition at least every hour in a chair, and shift weight every 15 minutes if they can
  • Inspect the skin at every reposition, and at every shift change
  • A Braden score of 18 or less flags risk, and a lower score means higher risk
  • Keep skin clean and dry, and use a barrier product where there is moisture
  • Protein, calories and fluid, because a wound cannot heal without material

Never massage a reddened area over a bony point. It damages the tissue underneath.

Where they form

Bone under skin with nothing in between. Sacrum, heels, hips, elbows, shoulder blades, back of the head.

In a side-lying patient the ear and the greater trochanter. In anyone on a device, the skin under the tubing, the mask or the cast edge.

The heel and the sacrum account for most of what you will see.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
If You Cannot See the Base, You Cannot Stage It
Slough or eschar covering the wound makes it unstageable, not stage 3 or 4. And intact purple skin is a deep tissue injury, not a stage 1, because the damage began below the surface and is still hidden.
Bottom Line: Stage by depth and by what you can see. Turn every 2 hours, float the heels, never massage the red.
09

What you do first

Every option here is something a nurse might reasonably do. Order is the whole question.

The ordering rules
  • Assess tolerance before you move anyone. Dizziness, pain and pressure come first
  • Safety of the environment before the technique. Locked wheels, low bed, clear floor
  • Equipment and extra people before your own back
  • If the patient becomes dizzy or weak mid-transfer, sit or lower them where they are
  • A device that does not fit is fixed before the patient walks with it
  • Prevention beats explanation. Set the turning schedule rather than telling them to move
Answers that are almost always wrong
  • Standing a patient straight up from lying flat
  • Lifting under the arms or by the axillae
  • Standing on the patient's strong side
  • Putting the cane in the hand on the injured side
  • Leading with the weak leg going up stairs
  • Massaging a reddened bony area
  • Using a donut ring under the sacrum
  • One nurse lifting a dependent patient alone

Learn this list. It eliminates two options in most questions on this topic.

After hip surgery, specifically

Posterior approach precautions come up constantly, so keep them separate in your head.

  • No bending the hip past 90 degrees, so raised seats and no low chairs
  • No crossing the legs or the ankles, and an abduction pillow between the knees
  • No turning the toes inward
  • Sudden severe pain, a shortened leg and outward rotation means dislocation. Stop and call
Bottom Line: Assess, secure the environment, get help, then move. When something goes wrong mid-transfer, lower them where they stand.
