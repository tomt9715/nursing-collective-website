01

What actually gets you

Pain management looks like a comfort topic. It is a safety topic wearing comfort clothing.

Almost nobody loses points on what morphine is. Points go here, in four predictable ways.

You watched the respiratory rate and missed the sedation level

This is the single most important safety point in the topic. Sedation increases first. Respiratory depression follows it.

A rising sedation score is your warning. By the time the rate drops, the warning has already been ignored. Check how rousable the patient is before every dose, not just the number on the monitor.

You treated the number instead of the patient

A score of 7 does not name a dose. It names a starting point.

The dose depends on the patient in front of you. Age, renal function, opioid history, sedation level and what else they are taking all move it. Two patients reporting 7 can need very different plans.

You disbelieved a patient whose vital signs looked normal

Chronic pain does not raise the heart rate. The body adapts within days.

So a patient with a normal pulse, a normal pressure and no grimace can be in severe pain. Tolerance does the same thing. The textbook face is a picture of new acute pain, not of pain in general.

You gave a bigger dose for a brand new problem

Pain that is out of proportion, unrelieved by opioids and worse on passive stretch is compartment syndrome until proven otherwise.

Medicating it away removes the only warning you had. This is the one time escalating the analgesic is the wrong answer. You assess and you notify.

Bottom Line: Study this topic as three questions, not one. What kind of pain is this. What does this patient tolerate. And how sedated are they right now.
02

The four things

Four classes carry this topic. For each one you should be able to say four things with the book closed.

What it is in a sentence. When you reach for it. What you monitor, or where the ceiling sits. And the teaching point.

If you cannot say all four out loud, you do not know it yet. Familiarity is not recall.

Class What it is When you reach for it Monitor or ceiling Teaching point
Acetaminophen A non-opioid analgesic and antipyretic with no anti-inflammatory effect. Mild pain, and as the scheduled base under everything else. Hard ceiling of 4 g per day in a healthy adult; far lower with liver disease. Count every source. It hides inside combination opioids and cold remedies.
NSAIDs Anti-inflammatory analgesics that block prostaglandin production. Inflammatory, bone and post-operative pain. Gastrointestinal bleeding, kidney function, platelet function, blood pressure. Take with food. Report black stools, and any new swelling or falling urine output.
Opioids Agonists at opioid receptors that dull the perception of pain. Moderate to severe pain, acute or at the end of life. Sedation level first, then respiratory rate. No dose ceiling for pure agonists. Constipation never wears off. The bowel regimen starts with the first dose.
Adjuvants Drugs whose main job is something else, used here for pain. Neuropathic pain, and to spare opioid in any plan. Sedation and dizziness. Gabapentin needs a renal dose adjustment. Burning, shooting nerve pain answers to these, not to more opioid.
Copy this down

Four rows on one index card, by hand. Acetaminophen has a ceiling. NSAIDs have organs to protect. Opioids have a sedation score. Adjuvants own nerve pain. That card is most of the exam.

03

Assessment, and picking the tool

Discrimination axis: can this patient tell you.

That one question picks the tool. Everything else follows from it.

Self-report is the standard. Not your observation, not the vital signs, not what the last shift wrote.

ToolWho it is forHow it worksScale
Numeric rating scaleVerbal adults, and children 8 and overSelf-report0 to 10, where 0 is none and 10 is the worst imaginable
Wong-Baker FACESAge 3 and up, including adults who struggle with a number lineSelf-report0 to 10, across 6 faces. Say no hurt to hurts worst, not happy to sad
FLACCInfants and nonverbal children, 2 months to 7 yearsBehavioural observation0 to 10. Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability, each scored 0 to 2
CPOTIntubated or unconscious adults in intensive careBehavioural observation0 to 8. Facial expression, body movement, muscle tension, ventilator compliance
AbbeyPatients with advanced dementiaBehavioural observation0 to 18, across vocalisation, expression, body language and physical change
PAINADAdvanced dementia and nonverbal adultsBehavioural observation0 to 10, across breathing, vocalisation, facial expression, body language and consolability
Self-report outranks your observation

If a patient says 8 out of 10 while smiling and chatting, the answer is 8 out of 10. People cope with pain differently. Some go quiet, some laugh, some cry.

The behavioural tools are for patients who cannot report. They are not there to check up on patients who can.

The patient who cannot speak

Intubated, sedated, or too young. You still owe them an assessment.

Work through it in order, and document what you used.

  1. 1
    Try for self-report anyway. An intubated patient may nod, squeeze a hand, or point at a scale. A yes or no answer is still self-report.
  2. 2
    Look for a cause. Surgery, a fracture, a full bladder, a dressing change, turning. If a procedure would hurt you, assume it hurts them.
  3. 3
    Use a behavioural tool. CPOT for the intubated adult, PAINAD or Abbey for advanced dementia, FLACC for the small child. Score it, do not eyeball it.
  4. 4
    Ask the family. They know this person's baseline. A caregiver noticing that the patient is not themselves is real data.
  5. 5
    Try an analgesic trial. Give a dose as prescribed and watch. Behaviour that settles afterwards was pain.

PQRST, for the story

PQRST
  • Provokes and palliates. What brings it on and what eases it
  • Quality. Their words. Burning and stabbing point somewhere different from aching
  • Radiates. Where it travels
  • Severity. The number, on the right scale
  • Timing. Onset, duration, pattern

The Q and the R do most of the diagnostic work here. Quality tells you which drug class to reach for.

The step that gets skipped

Assessment without reassessment is not finished. After giving analgesia:

  • 45 to 60 minutes after an oral dose, because oral drugs have not peaked at 30
  • 15 to 30 minutes after an intravenous dose

Those intervals match when each route peaks. Document the score before, the intervention, and the score after.

The target is usually not zero. Agree a comfort-function goal instead: the level at which they can breathe deeply, cough and walk.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Normal Vitals Are Not Evidence
Sympathetic signs fade within days, so chronic pain and tolerance both look calm. When a question offers you an answer that starts with doubting the patient, it is almost never correct. The exception is not disbelief; it is noticing that this pain is a new problem rather than the old one.

Acute or chronic

Discrimination axis: has the body had time to adapt.

Acute: a warning signal
  • Identifiable cause: surgery, injury, a procedure
  • Resolves as the tissue heals
  • Early sympathetic signs: fast pulse, high pressure, sweating
  • The patient looks like they are in pain

Goal: control it fast, and get them moving and breathing deeply.

Chronic: past its usefulness
  • Lasts beyond expected healing, commonly past 3 months
  • Vital signs are normal because the body adapted
  • Depression, poor sleep, fatigue, withdrawal from activity
  • The patient may look entirely comfortable

Goal: function, not a zero. Multimodal and non-drug measures carry more of the load.

The axis that separates them

Time, and whether the vital signs still react. Acute pain still shouts through the sympathetic nervous system. Chronic pain has gone quiet without going away.

That is why normal vitals rule out nothing. A stem that gives you a calm patient with a score of 8 is testing exactly this.

Nociceptive or neuropathic

Discrimination axis: intact nerves reporting damage, or damaged nerves misfiring.

This axis picks the drug. Get it wrong and you escalate an opioid that was never going to work.

Nociceptive: tissue is damaged

Somatic. Bone, joint, muscle, skin. Sharp, aching, well localised. The patient points to it.

Visceral. Organs. Dull, cramping, squeezing, poorly localised. It refers to odd places.

Answers to: acetaminophen, NSAIDs and opioids.

Neuropathic: the nerve is damaged

Words to listen for. Burning, shooting, electric, tingling, pins and needles, numb and painful at once.

Where it comes from. Diabetic neuropathy, shingles pain that outlasts the rash, phantom limb pain, sciatica, chemotherapy.

Answers to: gabapentin or pregabalin, tricyclics such as amitriptyline, duloxetine. Opioids alone work poorly.

The axis that separates them

The adjective the patient chose. Aching, throbbing and cramping are nociceptive. Burning, shooting and electric are neuropathic.

When a stem hands you burning foot pain in a diabetic patient, the correct answer is an adjuvant. More opioid is the distractor.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Burning Means Adjuvant
Burning, shooting and electric are the three words that move you off the opioid ladder and onto gabapentin, pregabalin, a tricyclic or duloxetine. Listen for the adjective before you look at the dose. Nerve pain that keeps climbing on opioids is not undertreated; it is treated with the wrong class.
Bottom Line: Can they tell you decides the tool. Self-report beats observation. Reassess at 45 to 60 minutes oral, 15 to 30 minutes IV, and chart both scores.
04

Non-opioids and their ceilings

Discrimination axis: which organ pays for this drug.

Non-opioids are not the weak option. They are the floor everything else stands on.

Both of them have a ceiling. That is the difference from an opioid, and it is what questions test.

Acetaminophen

It treats pain and fever. It does nothing for inflammation.

The liver is what pays. Overdose is the leading cause of acute liver failure in adults.

PatientUsual daily ceilingWhy it moves
Healthy adult4 g per day, and many facilities set 3 gThe margin between a therapeutic day and a toxic one is narrow
Liver diseaseLower, commonly 2 g per day or lessA damaged liver cannot clear the toxic metabolite
Chronic alcohol useLower, commonly 2 g per day or lessAlcohol depletes the glutathione that neutralises the metabolite
Frail older adult, low weightLower, and check the prescriptionLess reserve, more drugs, thinner margin
The hidden acetaminophen

Most accidental overdoses are not one big dose. They are three ordinary sources added together.

Acetaminophen sits inside oxycodone and hydrocodone combination tablets. It sits inside over-the-counter cold and flu remedies and sleep aids. It sits inside the plain tablet the patient takes at home.

Before you give a combination opioid, add up the day's total. If the patient needs more opioid and is at the ceiling, the answer is a single-agent opioid, not another combination tablet.

The antidote is acetylcysteine, and it works best given early.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Add Up Every Source
When a stem lists a combination opioid plus a scheduled acetaminophen plus a cold remedy, it is asking you to add. The combination tablet is what caps the dose, not the opioid inside it. Switch to a single-agent opioid when you need more relief and the acetaminophen is spent.

NSAIDs

Ibuprofen, naproxen, ketorolac, aspirin. These work where there is inflammation.

They are excellent for bone pain, dental pain and post-operative pain. They also carry four risks worth memorising.

RiskWhat you watch forWho is most exposed
GastrointestinalEpigastric pain, black tarry stools, coffee-ground vomit, falling hemoglobinOlder adults, steroid or anticoagulant users, prior ulcer
RenalFalling urine output, rising creatinine, new edemaDehydration, heart failure, existing kidney disease, ACE inhibitor plus diuretic
BleedingBruising, prolonged oozing, bleeding at a surgical siteAnticoagulated patients, low platelets, anyone heading to surgery
CardiovascularRising blood pressure, fluid retention, chest symptomsExisting heart disease, hypertension, recent bypass surgery
Ketorolac has a clock on it

Ketorolac is a potent injectable NSAID and it is limited to 5 days total, by all routes combined.

Past that the bleeding and kidney risk climbs sharply. It is a short bridge, not a maintenance drug.

Teaching that gets tested
  • Take with food or milk. Sit upright afterwards
  • Report black stools, coffee-ground vomit or new indigestion
  • Stay hydrated, and report a drop in how much you are passing
  • Do not combine two NSAIDs; the risks add and the relief does not
  • No aspirin for a child with a viral illness, because of Reye syndrome
Bottom Line: Acetaminophen costs you liver. NSAIDs cost you stomach, kidney, platelets and pressure. Know which organ is already struggling and the choice makes itself.
05

Opioids

Pure opioid agonists have no ceiling dose. You titrate to effect and to side effects.

That freedom is exactly why the monitoring matters so much.

Morphine
Opioid, the reference drug

The standard against which the others are compared. Oral, IV, and long-acting forms.

Watch: active metabolites build up in kidney impairment. Histamine release causes itching and can drop the pressure.

Hydromorphone
Opioid, alternative

Several times more potent than morphine milligram for milligram, so the numbers on the order look small.

Watch: a decimal point error here is a large error. Check the dose twice.

Fentanyl
Opioid, fast and short by IV

IV onset is almost immediate and the effect is brief, which suits procedures.

Watch: the patch is for stable chronic pain in opioid-tolerant patients only. Never for acute pain, never opioid-naive. Heat raises absorption, so no heating pads, and report fever.

Oxycodone
Opioid, oral

Common for moderate to severe pain, alone or combined with acetaminophen.

Watch: the combination product is what caps the daily acetaminophen. Long-acting forms are swallowed whole, never crushed.

Codeine
Opioid, weak and unpredictable

It is a prodrug, so how well it works depends on the patient's enzymes.

Watch: not given to children under 12, and not after tonsil or adenoid surgery. Ultra-rapid metabolisers have died from ordinary doses.

Meperidine
Opioid, avoided

It breaks down into normeperidine, a neurotoxic metabolite that lowers the seizure threshold.

Watch: the metabolite accumulates in older adults and in kidney impairment. Reach for morphine or hydromorphone instead.

Meperidine is the wrong answer

Naloxone does not reverse normeperidine, and a seizure is not a side effect you titrate around.

If a stem offers meperidine for repeated or severe pain, it is a distractor. This is true of sickle cell crisis in particular, where dosing is frequent and the metabolite piles up.

Side effects, and which one never leaves

EffectDoes tolerance developWhat you do
ConstipationNo, neverStart a stimulant laxative with the first dose. Add fluid, fibre and movement
SedationYes, over daysScore it every time. Treat a rising score as a warning, not a comfort
Respiratory depressionYes, over daysHighest risk in the opioid-naive patient in the first 24 hours
Nausea and vomitingYes, usually within daysAntiemetic as prescribed. Do not abandon the opioid over it
ItchingYes, usuallyOften histamine, not allergy. Antihistamine, and reassess before labelling an allergy
Urinary retentionYes, usuallyCheck for a distended bladder if output stops. Bladder scan
Orthostatic hypotensionYesStand them slowly. Fall precautions, especially in older adults
The bowel regimen starts with the opioid

Every other opioid side effect fades. Constipation does not, at any dose, at any duration.

So it is prevented, not treated. A stimulant laxative goes on the chart the same day the opioid does, with fluid, fibre and movement alongside.

Waiting for the patient to report it is already too late. Ask about the last bowel movement on every shift.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Constipation Never Wears Off
Tolerance rescues you from the nausea, the itching and the drowsiness. It never rescues you from the constipation. When a question asks what to include in the plan of care for a new opioid, the laxative is the answer that separates a nurse who has done this from one who has read about it.
Scheduled beats as-needed for predictable pain

Pain is easier to prevent than to chase. When pain is continuous and expected, scheduled dosing controls it with less total drug.

That is why a sickle cell vaso-occlusive crisis is treated with scheduled opioids rather than as-needed dosing. The same logic applies to the first day or two after major surgery, and to cancer pain.

As-needed dosing is for breakthrough pain on top of a scheduled base, not as the whole plan.

Bottom Line: No ceiling, so the safety comes from monitoring rather than from the number. Sedation score first, bowel regimen from day one, and meperidine stays off the list.
06

Sedation comes first

Discrimination axis: how rousable is this patient, right now.

If you remember one thing from this guide, remember the order.

Sedation rises before breathing falls

Opioid-induced respiratory depression is almost never the first sign. Advancing sedation is.

The patient gets drowsy, then hard to rouse, then their breathing slows. Watching only the respiratory rate means you catch it at the last step instead of the first.

So the sedation level is assessed before every dose and at every check. It is the earliest warning you get.

Sedation scoring, and what each level means you do

Most facilities use a scored sedation scale beside the pain score. The letters differ; the actions do not.

LevelWhat you seeWhat you do
SNormal sleep, rouses easilyAcceptable. Continue as prescribed
1Awake and alertAcceptable. Continue as prescribed
2Slightly drowsy, easily rousedAcceptable. Keep monitoring
POSS 3Frequently drowsy, arousable, drifts off during conversationNot acceptable. Decrease the opioid dose 25 to 50 percent, notify the prescriber, monitor closely, add supplemental oxygen if indicated
POSS 4Somnolent, minimal or no response to verbal or physical stimulationEmergency. Stop the opioid, stay with the patient, stimulate, call for help, give naloxone as prescribed
The respiratory numbers

Count a full minute, and count breaths rather than trusting the monitor.

  • Under 12 breaths per minute: hold the next dose and reassess. This is a facility and exam convention, not a physiologic line; some programs use 10. The sedation score is the earlier signal
  • Rate is not the whole assessment. Depth, regularity and quality count too. Shallow, irregular or snoring breathing at a normal rate is still respiratory depression
  • Under 8 with a patient you cannot rouse: this is naloxone territory. Call for help
  • Shallow breaths at a normal count still count as depression. Watch depth, not just rate

Your facility sets the exact hold parameter. Know that it exists and look it up before your first shift.

Who is most at risk

The risk stack
  • Opioid-naive, and inside the first 24 hours of therapy
  • Obstructive sleep apnea, snoring, obesity
  • Older adults, and anyone frail or low weight
  • Another sedating drug on board, especially a benzodiazepine
  • Kidney or liver impairment, so the drug and its metabolites linger
  • A continuous infusion running alongside as-needed doses

Stack two or more of these and the monitoring gets tighter, not looser.

Naloxone

Naloxone displaces the opioid from its receptors. It works within minutes.

It does not do this selectively. That is the part students miss.

What naloxone also reverses
  • The analgesia. The patient wakes up in full pain. Plan for that, and reassess
  • The tolerance protection. In an opioid-dependent patient it precipitates acute withdrawal: agitation, sweating, vomiting, cramping, tachycardia

It also wears off faster than most opioids, commonly in 30 to 90 minutes. The opioid can outlast it and the patient can sedate again, so keep monitoring and expect that repeat doses may be needed.

Naloxone does nothing for benzodiazepine sedation. If the patient is on both, reversing the opioid does not fix all of it.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Sedation Is the Early Sign
Sedation goes up, then respirations come down. Always that order. So the question that hands you a patient drifting off mid-sentence is not asking about comfort; it is asking you to hold the dose and call. And once naloxone is given, remember it is shorter-acting than the opioid, so the watch is not over when the patient opens their eyes.
Bottom Line: Score the sedation before you count the breaths. A patient who cannot stay awake through a sentence has told you already.
07

Patient-controlled analgesia

A PCA pump lets the patient give themselves a small preset dose by pressing a button.

The safety of the whole system rests on one fact. A patient too sedated to be safe is also too sedated to press.

Only the patient presses the button

Not the family. Not a visitor. Not you.

Pressing it for someone removes the only built-in safety the pump has. A sleeping patient does not need a dose; a sleeping patient is telling you they are comfortable, or that they are already sedated.

Teach this to the family on the first shift, in those words. A well-meaning relative pressing the button through the night is a real and documented cause of harm.

The parts of the order

SettingWhat it meansWhy it matters
Demand doseThe amount given each time the button worksSmall by design, so no single press is dangerous
Lockout intervalThe minimum time between delivered doses, typically 5 to 15 minutes for IV opioidsPresses inside the window are recorded but deliver nothing
Basal or continuous rateA background infusion that runs whether or not they pressRaises the risk of respiratory depression, particularly in the opioid-naive
Hourly or four-hourly limitA cap on total delivery in a set periodBackstop if the patient presses constantly
Loading doseA one-off dose to get on top of the painGiven by the nurse, not the pump, and reassessed

What you monitor

  1. 1
    Sedation score and respiratory rate. On the schedule your facility sets, and more often in the first hours and after any change to the settings.
  2. 2
    Pain score. Before and after, on the same scale each time.
  3. 3
    Attempts against doses delivered. Many attempts and few deliveries means the pain is outrunning the settings. Report it rather than adjusting on your own.
  4. 4
    The line and the pump. Site, tubing, settings checked against the order, and a second nurse for setup and changes where your facility requires it.
  5. 5
    Understanding. Can the patient explain what the button does and when to press it. If they cannot, PCA is the wrong route for them.
Who a PCA suits

They must be able to understand the concept and physically press the button. Confusion, low consciousness or a hand that cannot work the trigger all rule it out.

Children can use PCA well, usually from around 7 years old, once they can grasp the idea.

Naloxone and monitoring equipment stay available for anyone on one.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
No Proxy Pressing
Any answer option in which a family member, a visitor or a nurse presses the button is wrong, however kindly it is worded. The pump is safe because a sedated hand cannot press. Teach the family that letting the patient sleep is the correct action, and document that you taught it.
08

Tolerance, dependence, addiction

Discrimination axis: is this the body adapting, or the behaviour changing.

Three of these four are expected and harmless. One is a disease. Students blur them, and patients get undertreated for it.

Tolerance

The same dose does less over time, so more is needed for the same relief.

Physiologic and expected. It is not a warning sign, and it is not a reason to withhold.

Physical dependence

Stopping abruptly, or giving an antagonist, produces withdrawal.

Physiologic and expected. The response is a taper, not a lecture.

Addiction

Compulsive use and craving that continue despite harm. Use for something other than pain relief.

A disease, not a moral failing. Treat the pain and treat the disorder; both are real at once.

Pseudoaddiction

Clock-watching, requesting by name, appearing preoccupied with the next dose.

Caused by undertreatment. It resolves once the pain is actually controlled.

The axis that separates them

Ask one question. Is the behaviour aimed at relief, or at the drug.

Needing more, or withdrawing when it stops, is the body. Using despite harm, using for effect rather than relief, is the disorder.

And the separating test for pseudoaddiction is time. Treat the pain properly and watch. If the behaviour disappears, it was never addiction.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Clock-Watching Is Usually Undertreatment
A patient asking for the next dose 20 minutes early is far more likely to be in pain than to be addicted. Look at the interval, the dose and the reassessment scores before you look at the person. Sickle cell patients are labelled drug-seeking more than anyone, and that label has cost lives.
Bottom Line: Tolerance and dependence are the body doing what bodies do. Addiction is a diagnosis, not a suspicion. Pseudoaddiction is your prescribing showing up as behaviour.
09

Adjuvants and multimodal analgesia

An adjuvant is a drug whose day job is something else. Used for pain, it does work an opioid cannot.

Multimodal means several mechanisms at once. Less of each drug, fewer side effects, better relief.

AdjuvantExamplesBest forWhat you watch
GabapentinoidsGabapentin, pregabalinNeuropathic pain of any causeSedation, dizziness, unsteadiness. Dose is reduced in kidney impairment
Tricyclic antidepressantsAmitriptyline, nortriptylineNeuropathic pain, especially at nightDry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, orthostatic drops. Relief takes weeks
SNRI antidepressantsDuloxetineDiabetic neuropathy, fibromyalgia, chronic musculoskeletal painNausea early, blood pressure, mood changes
CorticosteroidsDexamethasonePain from swelling, nerve compression, bone metastasesGlucose, infection risk, mood, fluid
Local anestheticsLidocaine patch, nerve blocks, epiduralLocalised pain, and after surgeryNumbness, weakness, the block wearing off unnoticed
Muscle relaxantsCyclobenzaprine, baclofenMuscle spasm alongside the painSedation, falls. It adds to opioid sedation
Why multimodal wins

Pain is generated in several places at once. One drug can only block one of them.

Scheduled acetaminophen plus an NSAID plus a regional block means the opioid has less to do. Less opioid means less sedation, less constipation and less risk.

When a stem asks how to reduce opioid requirement after surgery, the answer is almost always another modality, not a smaller opioid dose on its own.

Non-drug measures that actually earn their place

Use these alongside, not instead
  • Cold. Early after injury, for swelling and acute inflammation. Never on a sickle cell crisis
  • Heat. Muscle spasm, stiffness, chronic aching. Never over a fentanyl patch
  • Positioning and repositioning. The cheapest intervention on the list, and often the first one
  • Splinting. A pillow held against the incision when they cough or move
  • Distraction, music, guided imagery, relaxation. Best for procedural and anticipatory pain
  • Massage and TENS. Useful adjuncts for musculoskeletal pain
  • Sucrose. For procedural pain in neonates

These lower the pain, they rarely remove it. Offering distraction instead of a prescribed analgesic is not an acceptable answer.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Add a Mechanism, Not a Milligram
When pain is not controlled, the reflex is to raise the opioid. The better move is usually to add a different mechanism: a scheduled non-opioid, an adjuvant matched to the pain type, a block, ice or repositioning. More mechanisms beats more milligrams, and it costs the patient less sedation.
10

Special populations

The drugs do not change. What changes is the margin you have to work in.

Older adults
  • Start low and go slow; clearance is slower and the reserve is smaller
  • NSAIDs carry more bleeding, kidney and cardiac risk here
  • Meperidine is avoided outright
  • Use PAINAD or Abbey if dementia has taken self-report away
  • Watch for falls, retention and constipation as first signs of too much

The trap: undertreated pain causes delirium too. Withholding is not the safe option.

Children
  • Doses are weight-based and checked twice
  • FLACC for infants and nonverbal children, 2 months to 7 years
  • Wong-Baker FACES from roughly 3 years; numeric from about 8
  • No codeine or tramadol under 12, and neither after tonsil or adenoid surgery
  • No aspirin during a viral illness, because of Reye syndrome

The trap: a quiet, still child may be guarding, not comfortable. Involve the parent.

Opioid-tolerant patients
  • Their home dose continues; acute pain relief is added on top of it
  • Stopping the baseline causes withdrawal and worse pain
  • They need more, not less, and often sooner
  • Mixed agonist-antagonists such as nalbuphine or butorphanol can precipitate withdrawal

The trap: reading tolerance as addiction and cutting the dose. That is the classic wrong answer.

End of life
  • Comfort is the goal, and it outranks the usual monitoring targets
  • Pure opioid agonists have no ceiling; you titrate to relief
  • Opioids also relieve breathlessness, which matters as much as pain
  • Scheduled dosing with breakthrough doses available

The trap: withholding out of fear of hastening death.

The fear that undertreats the dying

Giving enough opioid to relieve severe pain at the end of life is ethically and professionally right.

The intent is relief. A dose titrated to comfort in a dying patient is appropriate care, not a hastened death, and worrying about it is not a reason to leave someone in pain.

If a question offers you an answer that withholds comfort from a dying patient over that fear, it is the distractor.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Baseline Plus, Never Instead
A patient on long-term opioids who breaks a hip needs their usual dose and acute pain relief on top. Substituting one for the other gives you withdrawal and uncontrolled pain at the same time. If a stem tells you the patient takes an opioid at home, the daily dose is a clue, not a red flag.
11

What you do first

Priority questions hand you four reasonable actions and ask which comes first. All four are things a nurse would do.

That is the trick. You are being tested on ordering, not on correctness.

The ordering rules
  • Assess before you medicate. You cannot evaluate relief without a starting score
  • Check the sedation level before the next dose. Every time, not just when something looks wrong
  • Hold and notify. Never skip a dose silently; the prescriber needs to know it was held and why
  • New or changed pain gets assessed, not just medicated. A different pain is a different problem
  • Safety beats comfort when the two conflict, and the sedation score is where they conflict
  • What you can do now beats what needs an order. Repositioning needs no prescription

The sequence for a patient reporting pain

  1. 1
    Assess it. Score it on the right tool, and take the story with PQRST. Is this the same pain as before, or a new one.
  2. 2
    Rule out a new problem. A pain that has changed in character, site or severity needs assessment before analgesia. Compartment syndrome, a perforation and a clot all arrive as new pain.
  3. 3
    Check the safety data. Sedation level, respiratory rate, blood pressure, the last dose and the time of it. Then decide whether to give, hold, or call.
  4. 4
    Choose the right agent. Match it to the pain type and to the ceilings. Non-drug measures go alongside, not instead.
  5. 5
    Reassess and document. 45 to 60 minutes after oral, 15 to 30 minutes after IV. Chart the score before, what you gave, and the score after.
  6. 6
    Escalate if it is not working. Unrelieved pain is a reason to call the prescriber, not a reason to wait for the next scheduled dose.
The one time a bigger dose is wrong

Pain out of proportion to the injury, unrelieved by opioids, and worse on passive stretch of the muscles. In a limb, that is compartment syndrome.

Do not medicate it harder. Assess the limb, loosen anything constricting, keep it level with the heart rather than elevated, and notify immediately.

The same principle runs wider. Escalating opioid for pain that is escalating for a new reason hides the reason.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
New Pain Is a Finding, Not a Dose
Pain that changes character, moves, or stops responding to what worked yesterday is new information. Treat it as an assessment finding first. Chest pain in a post-operative patient, a rigid abdomen, a tense calf, a limb that hurts more on passive stretch; none of these are analgesia problems.
Bottom Line: Assess, check the sedation, give, reassess, document. When something does not fit that loop, the answer is assess or notify, not medicate.
