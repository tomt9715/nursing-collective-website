Patient Safety

The topic where the correct answer is almost never the most caring one. It is the one that keeps the patient in one piece, and then tells someone.

18-22 minutes
Difficulty Level

The Nursing Collective

By the end, without notes

  • Name two acceptable patient identifiers, and the one that is never acceptable
  • Say what you do in the first minute after finding a patient on the floor
  • State the restraint renewal times by age, and the monitoring interval
  • Explain why a PRN restraint order is never valid
  • Give the fire sequence and the extinguisher sequence, in order
  • Say what goes in an incident report, and what never goes in the chart
01

What actually gets you

Safety questions look easy. They are the ones people lose points on.

The reason is that every option is something a decent nurse might do. You are being asked which one comes first.

You picked the kindest answer instead of the safest

Comfort, dignity and reassurance are real nursing. They are almost never the priority action in a safety question.

The exam is asking what prevents harm in the next sixty seconds. Warm answers are the distractors, and they are written to be tempting.

You moved the patient before you assessed them

Finding someone on the floor triggers a reflex to help them up. That reflex is the wrong answer every time.

Assess first. Level of consciousness, injury, vital signs, then decide how they move. Lifting an undiagnosed hip fracture makes it worse.

You treated a restraint as a nursing decision

Restraints are a last resort with a legal structure around them. Orders expire, they cannot be written as needed, and monitoring is on a clock.

Every question here has a less restrictive option in it. That option is usually correct.

You wrote the incident report in the chart

The chart gets the facts. What happened to the patient. What you assessed. What you did.

The incident report is a separate internal document. Referencing it in the chart pulls it into the record and defeats its purpose.

Bottom Line: Study this topic as one question. Of these four reasonable actions, which one prevents harm right now.
02

The four things

Five domains carry this topic. For each one you should be able to say four things with the book closed.

What it covers. The scenario you will see. The priority action. And the rule that is easy to break.

Domain What it covers The scenario Priority action The rule people break
Identification Right patient for every drug, specimen and procedure. Two patients with similar names, or a patient who cannot answer. Two identifiers, checked against the order and the band. The room number is never an identifier.
Falls Prevention, and the response when one happens. A confused older adult found on the floor. Assess before moving. Then vital signs and neuro checks. Helping them up first.
Restraints Least restrictive care, orders, monitoring, release. A patient pulling at a line or an endotracheal tube. Try every alternative first, and document that you did. Accepting a PRN order, or letting one expire unrenewed.
Medication safety Rights of administration, high-alert drugs, unsafe abbreviations. An order for a look-alike drug, or a dose with a trailing zero. Clarify with the prescriber before giving anything. Giving it because it was written down.
Environment Fire, oxygen, equipment, seizure precautions, home hazards. Smoke from a room, or a patient seizing in bed. Rescue anyone in immediate danger. Protect the airway and the head. Restraining a seizing patient, or putting anything in the mouth.

03

Right patient, every time

Discrimination axis: is this identifier attached to the person, or to the place.

Two identifiers, every time, for every medication, specimen, transfusion and procedure.

They must belong to the person, not to the bed.

Acceptable
  • Full name, stated by the patient rather than confirmed
  • Date of birth
  • Medical record number
  • The identification band, checked against the order
  • A photograph in the record, where used

Ask openly. Say what is your name and date of birth, not are you Mr Jones.

Never acceptable
  • Room number or bed number
  • The name on the door or the whiteboard
  • Recognising the patient from yesterday
  • Another staff member pointing at the bed

Why: patients move rooms. Beds get swapped in the night, and the sign does not.

Before a procedure, everything stops

The universal protocol has three parts. The last one happens with everyone present and quiet.

  • Verify. The right patient, the right consent, the right imaging and the right equipment
  • Mark the site. By the person doing the procedure, with the patient involved and awake where possible
  • Time out. Immediately before the incision, the whole team confirms patient, procedure and site out loud

Anyone in the room can stop it. That is the point of it.

Labelling at the bedside

Specimens are labelled in front of the patient, not at the desk afterwards.

Blood for transfusion is the strictest version of this. Two people verify the patient and the unit. They do it at the bedside.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
The Bed Is Not the Patient
Any option that identifies someone by room number, bed number or the name on a door is wrong. So is asking a leading question a confused patient will simply agree with. Have them state it, then check the band.
Bottom Line: Two identifiers that belong to the person. Ask openly, check the band, label at the bedside.
04

Falls, before and after

Discrimination axis: are you preventing one, or responding to one.

The two halves have completely different answers, and questions test both.

Who falls

The risk stack
  • A previous fall, which is the strongest predictor of the next one
  • Confusion, delirium, dementia, or new sedation
  • Age over 65, weakness, unsteady gait, poor vision
  • Sedatives, opioids, antihypertensives, diuretics, and simply taking many drugs
  • Urgency, frequency, diarrhea, or anything that makes the bathroom a race
  • Orthostatic drops, low glucose, low hemoglobin
  • An unfamiliar room, poor lighting, and equipment tethering the patient to the bed

Two or more of these changes the plan. General precautions are no longer enough.

Preventing one

What actually works
  • Bed in the lowest position, wheels locked, brakes checked
  • Call light and personal items within reach before you leave the room
  • Purposeful hourly rounding: pain, position, toileting, possessions
  • Toileting on a schedule for anyone with urgency or confusion
  • Non-skid footwear, clear floor, adequate lighting at night
  • Gait belt and assistance for anyone unsteady, rather than an instruction to call
  • Review the medication list. The prescription is often the cause

Telling a confused patient to press the call light is not a fall intervention. They will not remember.

Side rails count as restraints

One or two rails up for mobility and positioning is standard care.

All four rails up is a restraint. The patient cannot get out of bed. It carries the whole restraint structure with it. Patients also climb over rails and fall further.

After one

  1. 1
    Stay with the patient and assess where they are. Level of consciousness, airway, breathing, obvious injury, pain, deformity. Do not move them yet.
  2. 2
    Call for help rather than lifting alone. If a head injury or a neck injury is possible, immobilise and wait.
  3. 3
    Vital signs and neurological checks. Repeat them on a schedule, especially in anyone taking an anticoagulant.
  4. 4
    Notify the provider and the family as your facility requires.
  5. 5
    Document the facts in the chart. What you found, what you assessed, what you did. Complete the incident report separately.
  6. 6
    Work out why it happened and change the plan. A fall with no plan change is a fall that repeats.
FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Assess Them Where They Landed
Every option that starts with helping the patient back to bed is wrong. Assess on the floor first. Anticoagulated patients get neurological checks even when the fall looked minor, because the bleed shows up hours later.
Bottom Line: Prevention is rounding, toileting and the bed height. Response is assess, help, vitals, notify, document. Never lift first.
05

Restraints, and everything you try first

Discrimination axis: is there any less restrictive option left.

A restraint is anything that limits free movement and the patient cannot remove.

That includes mitts and belts. It includes all four rails up. A drug given to control behaviour counts too.

What you try first, and document

A restraint is only defensible once alternatives have failed. Questions almost always include one that has not been tried.

  • Find the cause. Pain, a full bladder, hypoxia, low glucose, infection, withdrawal, a new drug
  • Move the patient closer to the desk, or use a sitter or a family member
  • Camouflage the line. Sleeves, dressings, tubing routed out of sight
  • Reorient, reduce noise, correct hearing aids and glasses, keep day and night distinct
  • Bed and chair alarms, low beds, floor mats
  • Toileting schedule and activity, so the urge to get up is met
The order rules
  • Never as needed. A PRN restraint order is never valid, because each episode needs its own assessment
  • An order is required before application, except in an emergency where it is obtained immediately afterwards
  • The order states the type, the reason, and the duration
  • For violent or self-destructive behaviour, a face-to-face evaluation happens within 1 hour of application
  • Renewal for violent behaviour: every 4 hours for adults, every 2 hours for ages 9 to 17, every 1 hour for children under 9
  • Restraints end at the earliest possible moment, and a new order is needed to restart
What you do while they are on
  • Assess every 15 minutes for violent or self-destructive restraint
  • Release, reposition, range of motion, skin care, toileting and fluids at least every 2 hours
  • Check circulation, colour, sensation and movement distal to every device
  • Two fingers should slide under the restraint
  • Tie with a quick-release knot, to the bed frame, never to a side rail
  • Never leave a restrained patient alone in a raised or locked position they cannot escape in a fire

Document the behaviour, the alternatives tried, the checks, the releases and the patient's response.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Find the Untried Alternative
Scan the options for the least restrictive one that has not been tried yet. It is usually correct. And if a stem hands you an order written as needed, the answer is not to apply it; the answer is that the order is not valid.
Bottom Line: Least restrictive first, never as needed, face-to-face within 1 hour, checks every 15 minutes, release every 2 hours.
06

Medication safety

Most drug errors are not exotic. They are the wrong patient, the wrong dose, or an order nobody questioned.

The rights, checked three times

Right patient, drug, dose, route, time, and then documentation, reason and response.

Check the label against the order three times. When you take it out. When you prepare it. And again at the bedside.

Never give a medication someone else prepared. Never chart it before it goes in.

High-alert drugs

These cause disproportionate harm when they go wrong, so they get independent double checks.

  • Insulin, and especially concentrated insulin
  • Heparin and other anticoagulants
  • Opioids, particularly by infusion or in a patient-controlled pump
  • Concentrated electrolytes, above all potassium chloride, which is never given undiluted or by push
  • Chemotherapy
  • Neuromuscular blocking agents

Independent means the second nurse calculates it alone. They are not told your answer first.

Abbreviations that cause harm
  • U for units, misread as a zero or a four. Write units
  • IU, misread as IV or as ten. Write international units
  • QD and QOD, misread for each other. Write daily and every other day
  • A trailing zero. 1.0 mg is read as 10 mg. Write 1 mg
  • A missing leading zero. Point 5 mg is read as 5 mg. Write 0.5 mg
  • MS, MSO4 and MgSO4, confused with one another. Write the drug name

An order containing one of these is clarified, not interpreted.

Questioning an order

The dose looks wrong. The drug does not fit the diagnosis. The order is unclear. Any of those and you do not give it.

Contact the prescriber. If the answer does not resolve it, go up the chain. Carrying out an unsafe order does not move the responsibility. It stays with you.

Telephone orders work the same way. Write it down. Read it back. Get it signed inside the required window.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Clarify, Never Interpret
When a stem gives you an ambiguous order, a dangerous abbreviation or a dose that does not fit the patient, every option that involves giving the drug is wrong. So is asking another nurse what the prescriber probably meant. You contact the prescriber.
Bottom Line: Two identifiers, three label checks, independent double check for high-alert drugs, and clarify anything ambiguous before it reaches the patient.
07

Fire, oxygen and the room itself

Two acronyms, in the right order, answer most environment questions.

RACE: what you do
  • Rescue anyone in immediate danger
  • Alarm: pull the alarm and call it in
  • Contain: close doors and windows
  • Extinguish, or evacuate, depending on the size

Order matters. People first, then the alarm, then the doors.

PASS: how the extinguisher works
  • Pull the pin
  • Aim at the base of the flames
  • Squeeze the handle
  • Sweep side to side

Aim low. Spraying the top of the flames does nothing.

Evacuating

Ambulatory patients first, then those who need a wheelchair, then bed-bound patients.

Move horizontally to the next fire compartment before you move down a floor. Never use an elevator.

Oxygen makes everything worse

Oxygen does not burn, but it makes everything else burn faster and hotter.

  • No smoking anywhere near it, and signage on the door and at home
  • No open flame, no candles, no gas stoves, no space heaters
  • No petroleum-based products around the face, and no wool or synthetic blankets that spark
  • Secure cylinders upright, and store them away from heat
  • Check electrical equipment for frayed cords, and use grounded outlets

Seizure precautions

During, and what never to do
  • Stay with the patient and call for help. Note the time it started
  • Protect the head, lower them to the floor if they are standing, and clear the area
  • Turn them to the side to protect the airway when you can
  • Loosen tight clothing at the neck
  • Never restrain the movements, and never put anything in the mouth
  • Afterwards: side-lying, suction available, oxygen available, reorient gently

Set the precautions up in advance. Padded rails, bed low, suction and oxygen ready at the bedside.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
People, Alarm, Doors, Fire
The order in RACE is the answer to most fire questions. Anyone in immediate danger comes out first, even before the alarm. And in a seizure question, every option that restrains the patient or puts something in the mouth is wrong.
Bottom Line: Rescue, alarm, contain, extinguish. Aim at the base. Oxygen turns a small fire into a large one.
08

When something goes wrong

Discrimination axis: does this belong to the patient record, or to the internal review.

An error has already happened. The question is what you do in the next ten minutes.

  1. 1
    Assess the patient. Always first. The error matters because of what it did to a person.
  2. 2
    Take corrective action. Vital signs, monitoring, an antidote if there is one, and stay with them.
  3. 3
    Notify the provider and the charge nurse. Immediately, not at the end of the shift.
  4. 4
    Document in the chart what happened to the patient, what you assessed, what you did, and their response. Facts only.
  5. 5
    Complete the incident report separately, and do not reference it anywhere in the chart.
The axis that separates them

The chart is a clinical record of the patient. The incident report is an internal quality document about the system.

So the chart says what happened. The patient received 10 units instead of 4. You assessed them. Glucose was checked. The provider was notified. It does not say an error was made.

Writing incident report filed in the chart is the classic wrong answer.

Sentinel events and near misses
  • A sentinel event is a safety event causing death, permanent harm, or severe temporary harm. It triggers a root cause analysis
  • A near miss reached nobody, and it is still reported. It is free information about a system that nearly failed
  • Root cause analysis looks for the system flaw, not the person. Look-alike packaging, understaffing, an unclear order set

Reporting is expected and protected. A culture where errors are hidden produces more of them.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Patient First, Paper Second
Every error question has an option about reporting and an option about assessing. Assessment comes first, every time. Reporting matters, but it comes after you know whether the patient is harmed.
Bottom Line: Assess, correct, notify, chart the facts, report separately. The chart never mentions the incident report.
09

Safety by age

The same principle, different hazards. Questions here are usually about teaching the family.

Infants and children

Match the hazard to the developmental stage. What the child can newly do is what will hurt them.

  • Infants sleep alone, on the back, on a firm surface, with nothing else in the crib
  • Rolling brings falls from changing tables. Never turn away, not even for a second
  • Crawling and mouthing bring choking and poisoning. Small objects, cords, cleaning products, medications
  • Toddlers drown in very small amounts of water, including buckets and toilets
  • Car seats rear-facing until at least age 2, back seat until at least 13
  • Set the home water heater no higher than 120 degrees Fahrenheit
  • Store medications and cleaners locked and high, and keep the poison centre number visible
Older adults
  • Remove throw rugs, cords and clutter. Most falls happen at home
  • Grab bars in the bathroom, a raised toilet seat, a shower chair, non-slip mats
  • Lighting at night from bed to bathroom
  • Review the medication list for sedatives, anticholinergics and anything causing orthostatic drops
  • Vision and hearing checks, and footwear that fits
  • Rise slowly, and sit on the edge of the bed before standing
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors tested twice a year

Undertreated pain raises fall risk. So do poor sleep and unrecognised delirium.

Bottom Line: For children, teach to the next milestone. For older adults, look at the medication list and the bathroom.
10

What you do first

Safety questions are ordering questions. Here are the rules that decide the order.

The ordering rules
  • Airway, breathing and circulation outrank everything else
  • Assess before you act, unless the situation is already an emergency
  • Immediate physical danger outranks documentation, reporting and teaching
  • Least restrictive option first, and prove the alternatives failed
  • Stay with the patient rather than leaving to fetch or to call, when someone else can go
  • Prevention beats explanation. Doing the safe thing beats telling the patient to
  • When an order is unsafe, clarifying it outranks carrying it out
Answers that are almost always wrong
  • Helping a fallen patient back to bed before assessing them
  • Applying a restraint before an alternative has been tried
  • Accepting a restraint order written as needed
  • Putting anything in the mouth of a seizing patient, or holding their limbs still
  • Documenting that an incident report was filed
  • Giving a drug from an unclear order and clarifying afterwards
  • Identifying a patient by room number or by asking a leading question

Learn this list. It eliminates two options in most questions on this topic.

Bottom Line: Assess, then act, then tell someone, then write it down. Kindness is real, and it is still not the priority action.

Bottom Line: Nobody will ask you to define safety. You will be handed four decent actions and asked which one comes first. Learn the ordering rules and the almost-always-wrong list.
