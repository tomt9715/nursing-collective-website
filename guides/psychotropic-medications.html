Psychotropic Medications

Four movement problems that look alike, two emergencies that look like each other, and one drug level where the gap between working and toxic is smaller than a rounding error.

The Nursing Collective

By the end, without notes

  • Tell the four extrapyramidal syndromes apart, and say what each one needs
  • Separate neuroleptic malignant syndrome from serotonin syndrome in one question
  • State the lithium therapeutic range, the toxic threshold, and when the level is drawn
  • Give the clozapine monitoring schedule and the count that stops the drug

01

What actually gets you

Nobody loses points on what an antipsychotic is for.

Points go in four places, and all four are look-alike problems.

You called akathisia anxiety

A patient who cannot sit still, three days into an antipsychotic, is not anxious. That is akathisia.

The difference matters because the answers go opposite ways. Anxiety gets reassurance and maybe a benzodiazepine. Akathisia is a drug effect that needs the prescriber, a dose change or a different agent.

Treating it as anxiety leaves the cause running, and akathisia is genuinely unbearable.

You could not separate the two hyperthermic emergencies

Both give you fever, confusion and a rigid patient on a psychiatric drug.

Three findings separate them every time. How fast it came on. What the reflexes are doing. What the rigidity feels like. Learn those three and the question answers itself.

You learned lithium as one number

Therapeutic tops out at 1.2 and toxicity starts at 1.5. That is the whole margin.

So the questions are not really about the number. They are about the things that move it: vomiting, diarrhea, sweating, a low-salt diet, a new diuretic, a new anti-inflammatory.

Anything that drops sodium or volume raises lithium.

You thought the risk drops as soon as the mood lifts

Energy returns before mood does. A severely depressed patient who was too slowed to act now has the capacity to.

So the first weeks raise suicide risk rather than lowering it. So does any sudden lift in a withdrawn patient.

Bottom Line: This topic is four discriminations, not a drug list. Which movement problem. Which emergency. What moved the lithium. And what the returning energy means.
02

The four things

Five classes carry this topic. For each one you should be able to say four things with the book closed.

What it treats. How long until it works. What you monitor. And the teaching point.

If you cannot say all four out loud, you do not know it yet.

Class What it treats Time to effect What you monitor Teaching point
Antipsychotics Psychosis, mania, agitation. Some are mood stabilizers too. Agitation settles in hours. Delusions and hallucinations take weeks. Movement, weight, glucose, lipids. Temperature if anything looks off. Report a stiff jaw, restless legs or any new movement the same day.
Antidepressants Depression, anxiety disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder, some pain. Some lift by 1 to 2 weeks. Full effect at 4 to 6 weeks. Mood, energy, suicidal thinking, especially early. Keep taking it once you feel better, and never stop abruptly.
Mood stabilizers Bipolar disorder, acute mania, maintenance. Days to a couple of weeks for mania. Drug levels, kidney and thyroid for lithium, liver and platelets for valproate. Steady salt and steady fluid. Illness that dries you out is a reason to call.
Anxiolytics Acute anxiety, agitation, alcohol withdrawal. Benzodiazepines work in minutes. Buspirone takes weeks. Sedation, respiratory rate, falls, and how long they have been on it. No alcohol, and never stop a long-standing benzodiazepine suddenly.
Stimulants Attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder, narcolepsy. Same day, often within the hour. Appetite, growth in children, sleep, heart rate and blood pressure. Give it early so sleep survives, and eat before the appetite goes.
Copy this down

Five rows on one index card, by hand. Then cover the last three columns and rebuild them. Time to effect is the column that answers the most questions, because most wrong answers are impatient.

03

Antipsychotics

Discrimination axis: does this drug cost you movement, or does it cost you metabolism.

That single trade-off separates the two generations, and it drives most of the questions.

First generation, typical

Names: haloperidol, fluphenazine, chlorpromazine, thiothixene.

  • Strong dopamine blockade, so they work well on hallucinations and delusions
  • They do little for the negative symptoms
  • Movement effects are the price. High risk of every extrapyramidal syndrome
  • Tardive dyskinesia risk is higher than with the newer agents
  • Low-potency agents such as chlorpromazine sedate more and drop the pressure
Second generation, atypical

Names: risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, aripiprazole, ziprasidone, clozapine.

  • They act on dopamine and serotonin, and they help the negative symptoms more
  • Fewer movement effects, though risperidone at higher doses behaves like a typical
  • Metabolic effects are the price. Weight, glucose, lipids
  • Olanzapine and clozapine are the heaviest on weight and glucose
  • Ziprasidone prolongs the QT interval, so know the potassium and the other drugs
The axis that separates them

Ask what this patient can least afford to lose.

A young person facing decades of treatment usually cannot afford tardive dyskinesia, which may not reverse. A patient with diabetes and obesity cannot afford olanzapine.

Neither generation is simply better. They fail in different directions.

What actually responds, and what does not

Set the expectation, or the patient stops the drug
  • Hours to days. Agitation, sleep, hostility
  • Two to four weeks. Hallucinations and delusions begin to loosen
  • Four to six weeks and beyond. The fuller antipsychotic effect
  • Least responsive. Flat affect, poverty of speech, no motivation, social withdrawal

Patients often stop at week two because the voices are still there. So tell them the timeline in advance. That is an intervention, not small talk.

Metabolic monitoring

Weight, waist, blood pressure, fasting glucose or A1c, and a lipid panel at baseline.

  • Weight and body mass index monthly for the first 6 months, then quarterly once the dose is stable
  • Glucose and lipids rechecked within the first few months, then at least annually
  • More often if the weight is climbing, or if diabetes or heart disease is already there

Exact intervals vary by guideline and facility. Know that baseline plus scheduled follow-up is the pattern, and that a rising weight brings the next check forward.

Long-acting injectables

Given every two to four weeks, or longer for some products, instead of a daily tablet.

They exist for one reason. Stopping the medication is the most common cause of relapse. A missed injection is visible, and a missed tablet is not.

An adverse effect lasts as long as the depot does. So the oral form is usually tolerated first.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Impatience Is the Wrong Answer
When a stem says the patient is still hearing voices after five days and asks what the nurse should do, the answer is not to raise the dose or to call it a failure. It is to continue and to explain the timeline. Agitation settles first, and the psychosis takes weeks.
Bottom Line: Typicals cost movement, atypicals cost metabolism. Agitation settles in hours, psychosis takes weeks, and negative symptoms respond least.
04

Four movement problems, told apart

Discrimination axis: how long has the patient been on the drug, and does it reverse.

All four come from dopamine blockade. Timing is what names them.

SyndromeWhenWhat you seeWhat you do
Acute dystonia Hours to days. Earliest of the four Sustained muscle spasm. Stiff jaw, twisted neck, arched back, eyes rolled upward. Can involve the larynx Emergency. Give the anticholinergic as prescribed, commonly benztropine or diphenhydramine, by injection. Stay with them and protect the airway
Akathisia Days to weeks Inner restlessness the patient cannot sit through. Pacing, rocking, shifting constantly Report it. Dose reduction, a change of agent, or a beta blocker or benzodiazepine as prescribed. Do not treat it as anxiety
Pseudoparkinsonism Weeks Shuffling gait, mask-like face, drooling, cogwheel rigidity, resting tremor, stooped posture Report it. Anticholinergic as prescribed, or a dose change. It reverses
Tardive dyskinesia Months to years Involuntary lip smacking, tongue protrusion, chewing, grimacing, sometimes trunk and limbs Report immediately. It may be permanent. Anticholinergics do not help and can worsen it
The two that get confused

Akathisia or anxiety. Ask what changed. Restlessness that started after an antipsychotic, in a patient whose thoughts are not racing, is akathisia. The patient often describes it as being in their legs rather than their mind.

Tardive dyskinesia or pseudoparkinsonism. Parkinsonism is too little movement. Tardive dyskinesia is too much, and it lives in the face and the mouth.

Giving benztropine for tardive dyskinesia is a classic wrong answer, because it can make it worse.

Screening for tardive dyskinesia

The Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale is the standard tool. It runs on a schedule, not only when something looks wrong.

Screen every 3 to 6 months while a patient is on an antipsychotic. Sooner if anything new appears.

Early detection matters because the odds of reversal fall the longer it runs.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Restless Legs Beat Racing Thoughts
When a stem gives you a newly medicated patient who cannot stop moving, check whether the distress is in the body or in the thoughts. Akathisia is a motor problem the patient describes physically. Any answer that offers reassurance and no report to the prescriber is wrong.
Bottom Line: Hours is dystonia, days is akathisia, weeks is parkinsonism, months is tardive. The first is an emergency and the last may not reverse.
05

The two hyperthermic emergencies

Discrimination axis: onset speed, reflexes, and the feel of the rigidity.

Both are febrile, confused, rigid patients on a psychiatric drug. Three findings split them.

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome

Cause: dopamine blockade. Antipsychotics, or abrupt withdrawal of a dopamine agonist.

  • Onset over days to weeks
  • Lead-pipe rigidity, a uniform resistance through the whole range
  • Reflexes are sluggish or normal
  • Pupils usually normal
  • High fever, unstable pressure and pulse, altered consciousness, sweating
  • Creatine kinase rises, sometimes enormously, and the kidneys are at risk

Do: stop the antipsychotic, cool them, fluids, monitor, and expect dantrolene or bromocriptine as prescribed.

Serotonin syndrome

Cause: too much serotonin. Often two serotonergic drugs together, or a dose increase.

  • Onset within hours, usually under 24
  • Clonus and hyperreflexia, most obvious in the legs
  • Tremor, twitching, agitation
  • Pupils dilated
  • Fever, sweating, diarrhea, overactive bowel sounds

Do: stop the serotonergic drugs, supportive care, and expect cyproheptadine as prescribed.

The axis that separates them

Three questions, in this order.

  • How fast. Hours points to serotonin. Days to weeks points to neuroleptic malignant syndrome
  • The reflexes. Brisk with clonus is serotonin. Sluggish is neuroleptic malignant syndrome
  • The rigidity. Lead pipe is neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Serotonin gives you twitching and clonus rather than uniform stiffness

The drug class in the stem usually confirms what those three already told you. Do not lead with the drug, because patients are often on both.

What you do first, either way

Stop the offending drug and get help. That is the first action in both, and it is usually the correct answer.

Then airway, breathing, circulation, continuous monitoring, active cooling, and fluids for the kidneys.

New fever and rigidity on an antipsychotic is an emergency until proven otherwise. It is not an infection to work up slowly.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Fast and Twitchy, or Slow and Stiff
Serotonin syndrome is fast and twitchy: hours, clonus, brisk reflexes, big pupils, loud bowel sounds. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is slow and stiff: days, lead pipe, sluggish reflexes. Say that sentence before you read the options and the question stops being hard.
Bottom Line: Hours and clonus is serotonin. Days and lead pipe is neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Both start with stopping the drug and calling for help.
06

Clozapine, on its own

Clozapine works when other antipsychotics have not. It is also the one that genuinely lowers suicide risk in schizophrenia.

It also carries risks no other agent in the class does, which is why it is never first line.

The neutrophil count

Clozapine can cause severe neutropenia, and the risk is highest in the first 18 weeks.

  • A baseline absolute neutrophil count is required before the first dose. Generally at least 1500 per microlitre, or at least 1000 for a patient with documented benign ethnic neutropenia
  • Weekly for the first 6 months
  • Every 2 weeks for the next 6 months, if counts stay normal
  • Monthly after 12 months, if counts stay normal
  • Severe neutropenia is a count under 500, and clozapine is stopped
  • A gap of more than 30 days off the drug restarts the schedule at weekly

Teach the patient to report fever, sore throat or mouth ulcers at once. Those may be the first clue.

What changed in 2025

The Food and Drug Administration removed the clozapine risk evaluation and mitigation strategy, effective June 2025.

Prescribers, pharmacies and patients no longer enrol in that program. Counts no longer have to be reported before a pharmacy can dispense.

Monitoring itself did not go away. The neutrophil count is still checked on the schedule in the prescribing information. What ended was the federal reporting machinery around it.

Textbooks printed before 2025 still describe the old program, so expect a mismatch between your book and current practice. If an exam asks, answer from the course material and know the real change.

The other four risks
  • Myocarditis. Highest in the first 8 weeks. New chest pain, breathlessness, fast pulse or flu-like illness gets reported the same day
  • Seizures. Dose related, so titration is slow and a lowered threshold is expected
  • Severe constipation. It can progress to bowel obstruction and it kills people. Ask about bowel movements routinely and start prevention early
  • Metabolic effects. Among the worst in the class for weight and glucose

Heavy drooling, especially at night, is common and is often what patients complain about first.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
A Sore Throat Is a Laboratory Order
In a patient on clozapine, fever or a sore throat is not a minor complaint. It is a reason to check the neutrophil count now. The same instinct applies to new chest pain in the first two months, which is myocarditis until disproven, and to a patient who has not moved their bowels in days.
Bottom Line: Weekly for 6 months, then every 2 weeks, then monthly. Under 500 stops the drug. Fever, chest pain and constipation are all reportable.
07

Antidepressants

Discrimination axis: what does this one do that the others do not.

The classes are similar in effect and very different in what they cost.

SSRIs
Fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram, paroxetine, citalopram

First line for depression and for most anxiety disorders. Safe in overdose compared with the older agents.

Watch: nausea and headache early, sexual side effects that persist, low sodium in older adults, bleeding risk with anticoagulants or anti-inflammatories.

SNRIs
Venlafaxine, duloxetine

Depression and anxiety, and duloxetine also treats neuropathic and musculoskeletal pain.

Watch: venlafaxine raises blood pressure at higher doses. Discontinuation symptoms are notably harsh, so tapering matters.

Tricyclics
Amitriptyline, nortriptyline, imipramine

Effective, and now mostly second line. Also used for neuropathic pain and for some other conditions.

Watch: anticholinergic load, orthostatic drops, and cardiac conduction. Dangerous in overdose, which matters in a suicidal patient.

MAOIs
Phenelzine, tranylcypromine, isocarboxazid

Reserved for treatment-resistant depression, because the interactions are extensive.

Watch: tyramine-containing food and many drugs can trigger a hypertensive crisis. Washout periods are required in both directions.

Bupropion
Norepinephrine and dopamine reuptake inhibitor

No sexual side effects, and it helps with smoking cessation. Activating rather than sedating.

Watch: it lowers the seizure threshold. Avoided in eating disorders and in seizure disorders.

Mirtazapine and trazodone
Sedating agents

Chosen when poor sleep or poor appetite is prominent. Mirtazapine increases both.

Watch: daytime sedation and weight gain with mirtazapine. Trazodone can cause orthostatic drops and, rarely, priapism, which is an emergency.

The window where risk goes up

Antidepressants carry a boxed warning for increased suicidal thinking and behaviour. It covers children, adolescents and young adults up to about age 24.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Energy, concentration and initiative return first. Mood and hopelessness lift later. So a patient who was too slowed to act becomes able to.

So watch closely in the first weeks. Watch again after any dose change. And a sudden calm in a previously agonised patient is assessed, not celebrated.

MAOI teaching, which is the tested part

Tyramine builds up when monoamine oxidase is blocked, and it drives blood pressure sharply upward.

  • Avoid: aged cheeses, cured and smoked meats, fermented foods such as sauerkraut and soy sauce, tap and craft beers, red wine, overripe or spoiled food, yeast extracts
  • The pattern: aged, fermented, cured or spoiled. Fresh food is generally fine
  • Report at once: sudden severe headache, palpitations, chest pain, stiff neck, nausea. That is a hypertensive crisis
  • Many drugs are also out, including other antidepressants, stimulants, and common decongestants in cold remedies

Washout: generally 2 weeks between an MAOI and most other antidepressants, in either direction. After fluoxetine it is about 5 weeks, because the drug and its active metabolite linger.

Stopping, and doing it badly

Abrupt discontinuation causes a syndrome of dizziness, electric-shock sensations, nausea, irritability, vivid dreams and flu-like aching.

It is not addiction and it is not relapse. It comes on within days of stopping. It settles when the drug is restarted or tapered properly.

Paroxetine and venlafaxine are the usual culprits because they clear fast. Fluoxetine rarely causes it because it tapers itself.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Energy Comes Back Before Hope Does
A depressed patient who suddenly seems brighter, starts giving things away or becomes peaceful after weeks of anguish is a higher risk, not a lower one. The safest reading of a sudden lift is that a decision has been made. Assess directly and ask about it.
Bottom Line: One to two weeks for a first lift, 4 to 6 for the full effect. Watch hardest early. MAOIs mean aged, fermented, cured and spoiled are off the table.
08

Mood stabilizers

Discrimination axis: which organ does this one ask you to watch.

Lithium

Still the reference drug for bipolar disorder, and the one with the strongest evidence against suicide.

Its problem is the margin. Therapeutic and toxic almost touch.

LevelWhat it meansWhat you do
0.6 to 1.2 mEq/LTherapeutic. Acute mania sits at the upper end, maintenance lowerContinue, keep monitoring
Above 1.5 mEq/LToxic. Coarse tremor, persistent vomiting and diarrhea, drowsiness, slurred speech, unsteady gaitHold the dose and notify immediately
Above 2.0 mEq/LSevere. Confusion, muscle twitching, seizures, irregular rhythm, reduced consciousnessMedical emergency. Supportive care, and dialysis may be needed
Anything that drops sodium or water raises lithium

The kidney handles lithium much as it handles sodium. Lose salt or volume, and it holds on to lithium too.

  • Vomiting, diarrhea, fever, heavy sweating, hot weather, strenuous exercise
  • A low-salt diet, or an abrupt change in how much salt they eat
  • Thiazide diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs
  • Dehydration from any cause, including a stomach bug

Teach steady salt and steady fluid. Teach them to call during any illness that dries them out. That teaching prevents more toxicity than the monitoring does.

Monitoring lithium
  • Levels are drawn 12 hours after the last dose, conventionally before the morning dose
  • Frequently while titrating, then every few months once stable
  • Kidney function, because lithium can impair concentrating ability over time
  • Thyroid function, because it can cause hypothyroidism
  • Pregnancy matters. Lithium carries a cardiac risk to the fetus, so it is a conversation before conception

Fine hand tremor is common and expected. A coarse tremor is a toxicity sign.

The anticonvulsant mood stabilizers

DrugWhat you monitorThe warning that gets tested
ValproateLevel, liver enzymes, platelets, ammonia if confusedLiver failure and pancreatitis. Highly teratogenic, including neural tube defects
CarbamazepineLevel, blood counts, sodium, liverBone marrow suppression and low sodium. Serious skin reactions, with higher risk in some Asian ancestries
LamotrigineSkin, at every visitAny rash stops the drug and gets reported now. Stevens-Johnson syndrome. Titration is slow for exactly this reason
The lamotrigine rash rule

Most rashes on lamotrigine are benign. A few are the start of a life-threatening skin reaction. You cannot tell them apart early.

So the rule is absolute. Any new rash, hold the dose and notify. Never wait to see how it develops.

That is why the dose is raised slowly over weeks. Restarting after a gap means titrating again from the bottom.

Pregnancy and people who could become pregnant

This conversation happens before conception, not after a positive test. By the time a pregnancy is confirmed, the neural tube has often already formed.

  • Valproate carries a high risk of neural tube defects and of impaired cognitive development
  • Carbamazepine also carries neural tube risk
  • Lithium carries a cardiac risk to the fetus
  • Untreated bipolar disorder in pregnancy carries its own serious risks, so stopping is not automatically safer

The answer is a planned discussion with the prescriber, contraception counselling and folic acid, not a unilateral stop.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Follow the Sodium and You Find the Lithium
Almost every lithium toxicity question hides a sodium or volume problem in the stem: a stomach bug, a new diuretic, a heat wave, a new diet, an anti-inflammatory for a sore back. Find that detail first and the answer is already chosen.
Bottom Line: Lithium is 0.6 to 1.2, toxic over 1.5, drawn at 12 hours, and moved by salt and water. Valproate is the liver, carbamazepine the marrow and sodium, lamotrigine the skin.
09

Anxiolytics and sedatives

Discrimination axis: does this work tonight, or does it work in a month.

That question decides which drug fits the situation, and it is what questions test.

Benzodiazepines

Names: lorazepam, diazepam, alprazolam, chlordiazepoxide, clonazepam.

  • Work within minutes, which is why they are used for acute distress
  • First line for alcohol withdrawal, where they prevent seizures
  • Tolerance and physical dependence develop, so they are meant to be short term
  • Sedation, falls and confusion, particularly in older adults
  • Added to alcohol or an opioid, they suppress breathing

Never stop abruptly. Withdrawal can cause seizures and can be fatal.

Buspirone
  • For ongoing generalised anxiety, not for a panic attack happening now
  • Takes 2 to 4 weeks to work
  • No sedation worth speaking of, no dependence, no withdrawal syndrome
  • Taken on a schedule rather than as needed
  • Avoid grapefruit juice, which raises the level

The teaching point: patients abandon it in week one because nothing happened. Say the timeline first.

The axis that separates them

Time. A patient in a panic attack cannot wait three weeks. A patient with chronic anxiety should not be on a benzodiazepine for three years.

Acute agitation in a stem points to a benzodiazepine. Long-standing generalised anxiety points to buspirone or an SSRI.

Hydroxyzine is the other non-dependent option for short-term use, and it sedates.

Flumazenil is not a routine answer

Flumazenil reverses benzodiazepine sedation. It can also precipitate seizures in a dependent patient, or after a mixed overdose.

So supportive care and airway management are the mainstay, and reversal is a considered decision rather than a reflex.

Compare that with naloxone for opioids, where reversal is the expected move. The two are not parallel.

FlorenceBot Pro Tip
Buspirone Does Nothing Tonight
Any stem that gives buspirone as needed for acute anxiety is testing whether you know it takes weeks and is taken on a schedule. And any answer that stops a long-standing benzodiazepine abruptly is wrong, because that withdrawal can kill.
Bottom Line: Benzodiazepines work tonight and create dependence. Buspirone works in weeks and does not. Neither is stopped abruptly without thought.
10

What you do first

Every option is something a nurse might reasonably do. Ordering is the question.

The ordering rules
  • A new fever with rigidity in a patient on an antipsychotic outranks everything else
  • Airway first. Acute dystonia involving the throat or tongue is an airway problem
  • Hold the dose and notify, rather than giving it and documenting a concern
  • Assess suicide risk directly whenever mood, energy or medication changes
  • A drug level is interpreted with the patient in front of you, not on its own
  • Teaching the timeline prevents more relapses than any adjustment you will make
Answers that are almost always wrong
  • Giving benztropine for tardive dyskinesia
  • Reassuring an akathisic patient without reporting it
  • Stopping an antipsychotic because it has not worked after a few days
  • Telling a patient to stop an antidepressant once they feel better
  • Stopping a long-standing benzodiazepine abruptly
  • Giving buspirone as needed for an acute panic attack
  • Continuing lamotrigine and watching a new rash
  • Treating a sudden calm in a severely depressed patient as improvement

Learn this list. It eliminates two options in most questions on this topic.

Why patients stop, and what to say

Non-adherence is the most common reason symptoms return. The reasons are concrete and answerable.

  • It has not worked yet. Give the timeline before they start
  • Side effects they were not warned about. Weight, sexual effects, sedation. Ask directly, because patients rarely volunteer these
  • I feel better so I do not need it. Explain that feeling better is the drug working
  • I miss the highs. A real and common reason in bipolar disorder, and worth naming honestly
  • Cost, complexity, or no insight. Simplify the regimen, consider a long-acting injectable, involve the team

Lecturing changes nothing. Finding which of those five is happening changes a lot.

Bottom Line: Fever and rigidity first, airway second, hold and notify third. Then ask why the patient wants to stop, and answer that.
